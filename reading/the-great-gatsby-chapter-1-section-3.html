The Great Gatsby — Chapter I, Section 3: The Light Across the Water (Abridged Reader)

Later that evening, Daisy and I went out on the porch alone. Daisy said we did not know each other very well, even though we were cousins. She told me she had been through a difficult time and had become cynical about almost everything. By that she meant that she found it hard to believe in people's goodness or in the promises they made. I thought of the telephone ringing while we tried to eat dinner.

I asked her about her little girl. Daisy told me that after the baby was born, she woke up feeling alone. Tom was nowhere nearby. When a nurse told her that the baby was a girl, she turned away and cried. She told the nurse she hoped her daughter would be a beautiful little fool. In this world, she said, perhaps that was the best thing a girl could be. I did not think Daisy actually wanted a foolish child. I thought she meant it might hurt less for a girl not to notice all the things she would be expected to accept.

Daisy laughed and claimed she knew how the world worked. Something about her expression changed, and I wondered whether she had told me a private truth or simply invited me to share in a performance. Perhaps it was both. Inside the house were money, comfort, and people who served dinner without making a sound. Out here, Daisy had just told me how lonely she had felt. Neither picture seemed to cancel the other.

When we went back in, Jordan was reading aloud to Tom. She put down the magazine and said she had a golf tournament the next day. Now I remembered where I had seen her before: in photographs. She was a well-known golfer. Daisy said Jordan would be spending many weekends with them and joked that she would arrange a marriage between us. Daisy would make sure we kept finding ourselves alone together.

As I got ready to leave, Tom and Daisy asked about a rumor that I was engaged to a woman back home. I said I was not. They seemed to think that if enough people had told them the story, it must be true. The truth was less clear than their version, though I had not made a promise to get married. In any case, I had come east to begin a different life, not to make my old neighbors' stories come true.

When I reached West Egg, I did not go inside at once. The night was warm, and the moonlight lay across the lawn. I saw that someone had come out of the shadow of my neighbor's mansion. A man stood alone, his hands in his pockets, looking toward the water. I felt sure he was Gatsby.

I almost called out to introduce myself. Then he stretched his arms toward the dark water, and I stopped. He looked as though he was reaching for something that he could see but could not touch. From where I stood, I thought he was trembling. I looked across the bay to see what had caught his attention. All I could make out was a single green light, small and far away, at what might have been the end of a dock.

When I looked back, Gatsby had disappeared. I was alone in the dark again. I did not know then what the light meant to him, or whether it meant anything at all. I knew only that a man with a house as large as his had stood at the water's edge, reaching toward something on the other side.

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

Vocabulary List

  • cynical /ˈsɪnɪkəl/ — Believing that people are mostly selfish and not good.
  • performance /pɚˈfɔrməns/ — Acting in a way that is not fully real or honest.
  • cancel /ˈkænsəl/ — To make something not true or not real anymore.
  • tournament /ˈtʊrnəmənt/ — A sports competition with many games.
  • rumor /ˈrumɚ/ — A story people tell that may not be true.
  • engaged /ɪnˈɡeɪdʒd/ — Promised to marry someone.
  • trembling /ˈtrɛmblɪŋ/ — Shaking a little because of cold, fear, or feeling.
  • disappeared /ˌdɪsəˈpɪrd/ — Went away so no one could see him anymore.
  • stretched /strɛtʃt/ — Reached out with your arms or body.
  • attention /əˈtɛnʃən/ — Careful looking or listening.
  • expression /ɪkˈsprɛʃən/ — The look on a person's face.
  • claimed /kleɪmd/ — Said something was true without showing proof.
