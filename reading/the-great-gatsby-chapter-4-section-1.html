The Great Gatsby — Chapter IV, Section 1: A Story on the Road (Abridged Reader)

On Sunday mornings, while church bells rang along the shore, guests returned to Gatsby’s garden. They stood among the flowers with drinks in their hands and told wild stories about their host. One woman said he sold liquor illegally. Another said he had killed a man. They repeated these things with the same easy confidence with which they asked for another drink. It did not seem to trouble them that none of them could prove what they said.

That summer I began to write down the guests’ names on an old train timetable. People came from East Egg, from West Egg, and from New York. Some belonged to old families, some worked in the movies, and others came to gamble. A man named Klipspringer turned up so often that people called him the boarder, as though he lived there. The paper eventually began to tear along its folds, but I could still read the names. They reminded me of how many people had enjoyed Gatsby’s generosity without learning a thing about him.

One morning late in July, Gatsby drove up to my cottage in his enormous cream-colored car. He had told me we would have lunch together, and he was taking me into the city. I had already seen the car, of course. Everyone had. Its polished metal caught the sunlight, and there seemed to be a box or a windshield wherever you looked. Gatsby dressed and spoke with great care, but he could never be still for long. Even that morning his foot tapped, and his hands kept moving.

Before we reached the village, he asked what I thought of him. I began an answer that did not really answer anything. He interrupted and said he wanted me to hear the truth about his life, rather than believe the rumors. His parents, he said, had been rich people from the Middle West, and they were all dead. He had studied at Oxford University in England because his family had a tradition of going there. He hurried over the name Oxford so awkwardly that I began to doubt him. When I asked what part of the Middle West he came from, he said San Francisco. Even that city seemed to be in the wrong part of his story.

He told me he had inherited money and traveled through Europe, collecting jewels and trying to forget a sad event. Then came the war. He said he had led soldiers forward in the Argonne Forest, had been promoted to major, and had received medals. I was struggling not to laugh at the grand story when he reached into his pocket. He handed me a medal from Montenegro with his name on it. Then he showed me a photograph of himself at Oxford, standing with several other young men and holding a cricket bat.

The medal and the photograph looked real. For a moment, the rest of the story seemed real, too. I could almost picture the jewels he had described. That was part of Gatsby’s effect on me: a moment earlier I had dismissed his story, and now I was supplying pictures of it myself.

He put the two objects away. There was a favor he wanted to ask, he explained, and he did not want me to think he was nobody. I expected him to tell me what he needed, but he only said that Jordan Baker would explain it at tea. I asked whether he was in love with Jordan. He said no. I had looked forward to seeing her for my own reasons, and now I was annoyed to discover that Gatsby had a plan for our afternoon.

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

Vocabulary List

  • confidence /ˈkɑnfɪdəns/ — A calm feeling that you can trust someone or something.
  • generosity /ˌdʒenəˈrɑsəti/ — The quality of being kind and giving to others.
  • gamble /ˈɡæmbəl/ — To play a game for money and risk losing it.
  • boarder /ˈbɔrdər/ — A person who lives in someone else's house and pays for food and a room.
  • polished /ˈpɑlɪʃt/ — Shiny and smooth because it has been cleaned and rubbed.
  • tradition /trəˈdɪʃən/ — A custom that families or groups have followed for a long time.
  • awkwardly /ˈɔkwərdli/ — In a way that is not smooth or comfortable.
  • inherited /ɪnˈherɪtɪd/ — Got money or property from a person who died.
  • promoted /prəˈmoʊtɪd/ — Given a higher and more important job.
  • medal /ˈmedəl/ — A small metal disc given as a prize or honor.
  • dismissed /dɪsˈmɪst/ — Decided that something was not important or not true.
  • annoyed /əˈnɔɪd/ — Feeling a little angry or unhappy about something.
