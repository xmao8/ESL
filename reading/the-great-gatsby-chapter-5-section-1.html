The Great Gatsby — Chapter V, Section 1: An Invitation to Tea (Abridged Reader)

When I got home that night, light covered the bushes and shone on the wires beside the road. For a moment I thought my cottage was on fire. Then I turned the corner and saw that the light came from Gatsby’s house. He had lit every floor from the lowest rooms to the tower. I expected another party, but there was no music or laughter. The wind moved through the trees, and the lights seemed to blink. As my taxi drove away, Gatsby crossed his lawn to meet me.

He said he had been looking into a few rooms, as though that explained lighting an entire mansion at two in the morning. Then he suggested that we drive to Coney Island. I said it was too late. Would I like to swim instead? He had not used the pool all summer. I told him I was going to bed. Still, he hung around, trying to look as if he were waiting for nothing.

I finally told him I had talked to Jordan. I would call Daisy and invite her to tea. Gatsby said he did not want to cause any trouble, but he did not leave. I suggested the day after tomorrow. He hesitated and said he wanted to get the grass cut. We looked down. There was a clear line where my untidy lawn met his neat one. I guessed it was not his own grass that worried him.

Then he asked whether I made much money selling bonds. I said I did not. He told me he had a small business on the side, the kind of work one did in addition to a regular job. He said I could make some extra money without spending much time on it, but the details had to stay private. It was clear enough when he made the offer: he seemed to think I ought to be paid for arranging the meeting. I was happy to do him the favor, but not on those terms. I told him I already had enough work. He assured me I would not have to do business with Wolfshiem. That was not my reason, I told him, and eventually he went home.

The next morning I called Daisy from my office. I invited her to tea and told her not to bring Tom. She pretended not to know who Tom was. I did not tell her Gatsby was coming.

On the day of the meeting it poured. At eleven, a man in a raincoat came to the door with a lawn mower. Gatsby had sent him to cut my grass in spite of the weather. That reminded me to fetch the woman who helped me with the housework. I went into the village to find her and buy cups, lemons, and flowers. I need not have bought flowers. At two, so many arrived from Gatsby’s house that I could hardly find a place for them.

Gatsby himself appeared an hour later. He wore a white suit, a silver shirt, and a gold-colored tie. He looked pale and had dark circles under his eyes. He asked whether everything was ready. I told him the grass looked fine, but by then he had almost forgotten the grass. He wanted to know when the rain would stop and whether we had everything for tea. We examined the twelve lemon cakes in my pantry as carefully as if the afternoon depended on them. He said they would do. For once his familiar way of calling me old sport did not sound easy at all.

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

Vocabulary List

  • mansion /ˈmænʃən/ — A very large and expensive house.
  • blink /blɪŋk/ — To close and open your eyes quickly.
  • hesitated /ˈhezɪteɪtɪd/ — Paused before doing something because you were not sure.
  • untidy /ʌnˈtaɪdi/ — Not neat or clean.
  • private /ˈpraɪvət/ — Not known by other people, or only for yourself.
  • offer /ˈɔfər/ — Something you say you will give or do for someone.
  • pretended /prɪˈtendɪd/ — Acted as if something were true when it was not.
  • poured /pɔrd/ — Rained very hard.
  • terms /tɜrmz/ — The conditions of an agreement.
  • pantry /ˈpæntri/ — A small room where you keep food in a house.
  • familiar /fəˈmɪliər/ — Well known to you because you have seen it before.
  • entire /ɪnˈtaɪər/ — The whole of something, with nothing left out.
