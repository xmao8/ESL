The Great Gatsby — Chapter VIII, Section 3: The Eyes Above the Road (Abridged Reader)

In the hours before daylight, Wilson told Michaelis what he had said to Myrtle before she died. He had taken her to the window and told her that she could fool him, but she could not fool God. Now, as Wilson looked out again, Michaelis followed his eyes. Across the road, on a faded advertisement, were the enormous eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg. They had looked down on the garage for years. Michaelis told him they were only part of an advertisement. Wilson did not seem to hear him. To Wilson, the eyes meant that someone had seen what he could not see.

Another man arrived to stay with Wilson, and Michaelis went home to get some sleep. When he returned, Wilson was gone. His movements were traced afterward. People remembered seeing him walking on the road toward West Egg. Somewhere along the way he found out Gatsby’s name. At the time I did not know who had told him. By the afternoon he was in West Egg, asking how to get to Gatsby’s house. He believed that the owner of the yellow car was responsible for Myrtle’s death.

While I was still away, Gatsby put on his bathing suit. He told the butler to bring him word if a telephone call came. Then he took a floating mattress out to the pool. The chauffeur, Gatsby’s driver, helped him fill it with air. The sunlight still looked like summer, although the gardener had already noticed that the leaves were beginning to fall. Gatsby had said that morning that he had not gone swimming once. Now he carried the mattress down through the trees to the water.

No telephone message arrived. I do not know what Gatsby thought as he lay on the water. Sometimes I think he had stopped expecting Daisy to call and knew that his old dream was gone. It is also possible that he was still listening for a voice from the house. I had watched him count on a future with Daisy even after the afternoon at the Plaza. It is hard to say what a hope like that feels like just before it ends. I know only what the people at the house could tell me and what I saw later with my own eyes.

The chauffeur heard shots, but he did not go to find out where they had come from. I took the train back from New York and went straight to Gatsby’s house from the station. The butler, the chauffeur, and the gardener came with me. Together we went down to the pool. At first the surface seemed almost still. Then we saw the mattress slowly turning in the water, moved by a small current.

Gatsby’s body lay on the mattress. We started toward the house with him. Then the gardener saw Wilson’s body in the grass a little way off. Wilson had found Gatsby at the pool, shot him, and then shot himself. I had been unable to reach Gatsby all day, but until I arrived I had kept thinking that I might still have another conversation with him. I had not known there would be no second chance to tell him what I meant at the gate.

When I saw both men dead, I thought of how much they had not known. Wilson had followed the yellow car to its owner without knowing who had driven it when it struck Myrtle. Gatsby had kept his telephone free without knowing whether Daisy meant to call. Their separate hopes and fears had brought them to the same quiet place. The rest of us arrived only after everything was over.

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

Vocabulary List

  • fool /ful/ — To trick someone into believing something false.
  • faded /ˈfeɪdəd/ — Having lost color or strength over time.
  • responsible /rɪˈspɑnsəbəl/ — The person who caused something to happen.
  • floating /ˈfloʊtɪŋ/ — Resting on top of water without sinking.
  • chauffeur /ʃoʊˈfɜr/ — A person whose job is to drive a car for someone else.
  • current /ˈkɜrənt/ — Water moving in one direction.
  • separate /ˈsɛpərət/ — Different and not together.
  • enormous /ɪˈnɔrməs/ — Very, very large.
  • mattress /ˈmætrəs/ — A soft pad that floats or that you sleep on.
  • surface /ˈsɜrfəs/ — The top of something, such as water.
  • bathing /ˈbeɪðɪŋ/ — Washing or swimming in water, as in a bathing suit.
