The Great Gatsby — Chapter II, Section 2: An Afternoon Above the City (Abridged Reader)

The apartment was on the top floor. Its rooms were small, but the furniture was large and heavy. To cross the living room, you had to pass close to chairs covered in cloth with pictures of ladies in a garden. A photograph hung on the wall. From too close, I thought it showed a bird on a rock. From farther away, I saw that it was the face of a woman in a large hat. It was a picture of Myrtle's mother.

Myrtle's first concern was the puppy. A boy who worked in the building brought a box with straw, some milk, and hard dog biscuits. Tom took a bottle of whiskey out of a locked cabinet. I have been drunk only twice in my life, and that afternoon was the second time. The apartment was still full of sunlight, but what happened there comes back to me as if I were looking through a cloud.

Myrtle called several people on the telephone. I went out to buy cigarettes. When I came back, Tom and Myrtle had gone into another room. I sat in the living room and tried to read a book someone had left there. I could not tell whether the book was confusing or I was. Then the doorbell began to ring, and I put the book down.

Catherine, Myrtle's sister, came in first. She had short red hair and wore so many bracelets that her arrival could be heard as well as seen. She looked at the furniture as if the place might belong to her, but when I asked if she lived there, she laughed. She shared a room with a friend at a hotel. The McKees, who lived downstairs, came next. Mr. McKee was a photographer. He had taken the picture of Myrtle's mother. His wife proudly told us he had photographed her 127 times since they got married.

By now, Myrtle had changed into a delicate cream-colored dress that made a sound every time she moved. She seemed to change her manner along with her clothes. She laughed louder, spoke as though she gave orders every day, and talked about being cheated by a woman who came to take care of her feet. When Mrs. McKee admired the dress, Myrtle said it was an old thing she put on when she did not care what she looked like. A moment later, she stood smiling while Mr. McKee considered how he could photograph her.

Catherine sat beside me and asked about West Egg. She had been to a party at Gatsby's house about a month before. She reported that people said he was related to the former German emperor and that this explained his money. I had no reason to believe her. Still, it was strange to hear so much confidence about a man I had seen only once and had not yet met.

Mr. McKee wanted somebody to introduce him to people on Long Island so he could get more work there. Tom suggested, with a laugh, that Myrtle introduce him to her husband. He even invented a title for a photograph of George by a gasoline pump. Then Catherine whispered that Tom and Myrtle should divorce their spouses and marry each other. She said Daisy was Catholic and that her religion was the only thing keeping them apart. I knew that Daisy was not Catholic.

The lie surprised me as much as Catherine's belief in it. She spoke as if the future had been settled and Tom and Myrtle were already planning to move west. All I could see for certain was an apartment crowded with furniture, people, and stories that did not quite fit.

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

Vocabulary List

  • furniture /ˈfɝnɪtʃɚ/ — Things like chairs and tables in a room.
  • delicate /ˈdɛlɪkət/ — Thin, soft, and easy to break or hurt.
  • cheated /ˈtʃitɪd/ — Treated unfairly by someone who was dishonest.
  • divorce /dɪˈvɔrs/ — The legal end of a marriage.
  • spouses /ˈspaʊsɪz/ — A husband or a wife.
  • religion /rɪˈlɪdʒən/ — A set of beliefs about God and how to live.
  • confidence /ˈkɑnfɪdəns/ — Feeling sure that something is true.
  • emperor /ˈɛmpərɚ/ — The man who rules a very large country.
  • crowded /ˈkraʊdɪd/ — Full of people and things with little space.
  • settled /ˈsɛtəld/ — Decided and fixed, with nothing more to change.
  • whispered /ˈwɪspɚd/ — Spoke very quietly.
  • invented /ɪnˈvɛntɪd/ — Made up something that was not true.
