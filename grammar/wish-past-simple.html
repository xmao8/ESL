Use of Wish and Hope

The word wish has many uses: wishes about the present, regrets about the past, good wishes to other people, and formal requests. It is often confused with hope — but wish and hope are different. Wish is for things that are not true, or cannot happen. Hope is for things that are possible. It is the perfect grammar for Thanksgiving: talking about the people you love — and the things you wish were different.

1. Wish + past simple — wishes about the present

Form: subject + wish(es) + subject + past simple.

  • I wish I lived closer to my family.
  • She wishes she had more time to cook a big dinner.
  • They wish they spoke English better at the dinner table.
  • He wishes he were at home for Thanksgiving.

The past form talks about now: I wish I lived closer means I do not live close now, but I want to.

With be, use were for all subjects in formal English: I wish I were, she wishes she were. In everyday speaking, was is also common: I wish I was at home.

2. Wish + could — wishes about ability

Use could (the past form of can) to wish you were able to do something now.

  • I wish I could thank my teacher in person.
  • She wishes she could cook turkey like her mother.
  • We wish we could visit our parents every week.

Could here is not the past — it is a wish about now: I cannot thank my teacher in person today, but I want to.

3. Wishes about other people

Put a different subject after wish to talk about someone else: I wish + he/she/they + past simple.

  • I wish my son called more often.
  • She wishes her children visited every month.
  • We wish the dinner started earlier, so the children are not so hungry.

This is a soft way to say you want someone to do something — kinder than a direct order.

4. Wish + would — a short extension: requests and complaints

Use wish + subject + would + base verb when you want someone to change a habit, or when something annoys you. It is a polite request or a gentle complaint.

  • I wish you would call more often. (= Please call more often.)
  • She wishes he would help with the dishes after dinner.
  • I wish it would stop raining on Thanksgiving Day.
Use would for habits and actions that can change. Do not use it for wishes about yourself — say I wish I lived closer, not I wish I would live closer — and do not use it for things that cannot change.

5. Wish + past perfect — regrets about the past

Use wish + subject + had + past participle to talk about the past — something you did, or did not do, and now you are sorry about it.

  • I wish I had called my grandmother last Thanksgiving.
  • She wishes she had not moved so far from her family.
  • We wish we had taken more photos at the dinner table.

The past perfect talks about a past that cannot change: I wish I had called means I did not call, and now it is too late.

6. Wish + noun — good wishes

Use wish + someone + noun to give a good wish to another person — for a holiday, a birthday, or good luck.

  • I wish you a happy Thanksgiving.
  • We wish you good luck on your test.
  • She wishes him a safe trip home.

You also see this at the end of a card or letter: Best wishes or With best wishes.

7. Wish + to-infinitive — a formal request

In formal English, use wish + to + verb to make a polite request. It means the same as want to or would like to, but it is more formal.

  • I wish to thank everyone for coming tonight.
  • He wishes to speak to the manager, please.
  • We wish to invite you to our Thanksgiving dinner.
In everyday speaking, use want to or would like to instead: I would like to thank everyone.

8. Wish vs Hope — what is the difference?

Hope is for something possible in the future — or something in the past that you do not know about yet. Use hope + present simple or hope + will.

  • I hope my family comes for Thanksgiving. (They might come.)
  • I hope you pass the test. (It is possible.)
  • I hope it will be sunny tomorrow.
  • I hope you had a good trip. (I do not know yet — maybe you did.)

Wish is for something that is NOT true now, or cannot happen. Use wish + past simple for the present and wish + past perfect for the past.

  • I wish my family came for Thanksgiving. (They are not coming.)
  • I wish I were rich. (I am not rich.)
  • I wish I had gone on the trip. (I did not go.)

The quick test: if it can still happen, use hope. If it cannot happen, use wish.

If only means the same as wish, but with a stronger feeling: If only I lived closer to my family!

Common Mistakes

  • Using the present form after wish: After wish, use the past form, not the present.
    Incorrect: I wish I live closer to my family. Correct: I wish I lived closer to my family.
  • Using “would” for a wish about yourself: Wish + would is for someone else’s habit, not for your own wish.
    Incorrect: I wish I would have more time. Correct: I wish I had more time.
  • Using “will” after wish: Never use will after wish for a present wish.
    Incorrect: I wish my son will call more often. Correct: I wish my son called more often.
  • Using “wish” for a possible future: If something can still happen, use hope, not wish.
    Incorrect: I wish I would pass the test. Correct: I hope I will pass the test.
  • Using “hope” for something untrue now: If something is not true and cannot happen, use wish, not hope.
    Incorrect: I hope I were rich. Correct: I wish I were rich.

Summary

  • Wish + past simple talks about a wish for now: I wish I lived closer to my family.
  • Wish + could talks about ability you want now: I wish I could thank my teacher.
  • Wish + someone else + past simple is a soft wish about another person: I wish my son called more often.
  • Wish + would is a polite request or complaint about a habit: I wish you would call more often.
  • Wish + past perfect is a regret about the past: I wish I had called my grandmother.
  • Wish + noun gives a good wish to someone: I wish you a happy Thanksgiving.
  • Wish + to-infinitive is a formal request: I wish to thank everyone.
  • Hope is for something possible; wish is for something not true or impossible: I hope my family comes (they might) / I wish my family came (they are not coming).

Practice by making your own “I wish …” and “I hope …” sentences about Thanksgiving — the people you love, the food you miss, and the things you are thankful for.
