The Great Gatsby — Chapter VII, Section 3: The Yellow Car (Abridged Reader)

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

Later I learned what had happened at Wilson’s garage before Tom, Jordan, and I got there. A neighbor named Michaelis owned a café next to the garage. George had locked Myrtle upstairs, saying they would leave in a few days. That evening Michaelis heard the couple fighting. Then Myrtle rushed into the road. Michaelis saw a car coming from New York. It hit her and drove on without stopping. Another car pulled over. By the time anyone could help Myrtle, she was dead.

Tom, Jordan, and I were driving back in the blue car when we saw the crowd and stopped. Tom thought it was an ordinary accident. He pushed inside the garage and caught sight of Myrtle’s body. A policeman was taking down what the witnesses said. One man described the car that had struck her as large and yellow. Wilson heard that and began crying out. He remembered the yellow car Tom had driven that afternoon. Tom bent over him and insisted that the yellow car did not belong to him and that he had not been driving it that evening.

When Tom put us back in the blue car, he drove away slowly at first, without speaking. Then he began to cry. He was sure Gatsby had run Myrtle down without even stopping. I heard the grief in his voice, but I also remembered Gatsby and Daisy leaving together. None of us had seen who was at the wheel. We reached the Buchanans’ house, and Tom offered to have a taxi come for me. Jordan asked me to come inside, but I wanted to be alone. I waited outside.

In the dark I saw Gatsby standing near the edge of the drive. He had come back to be near Daisy. I asked him who had been driving. He said Daisy had been at the wheel, though he planned to say he had been driving if anyone asked. Gatsby said Daisy had swerved when Myrtle rushed into the road. After the collision, Gatsby had taken over the driving. The car had not stopped. He was more worried about what might happen to Daisy than about anything else I told him.

Gatsby said he would wait outside until he was sure Tom would not hurt her. Daisy could turn her light off and on if she needed him. I went closer to the house to find out what was happening. Through a gap in the curtains I could see Tom and Daisy sitting across from each other at the kitchen table. There was food between them, but neither one was eating. Tom was speaking quietly, with his hand over hers. She looked up at him from time to time. They did not look happy, exactly, but they looked as if they understood each other.

I went back outside. Gatsby wanted to know if there was trouble. I told him it was quiet and asked him to come home with me. He said he would stay until Daisy went to bed. I did not know how to tell him that the scene in the kitchen had looked nothing like the scene he was trying to protect her from. Just a few hours earlier he had asked Daisy to say that her life with Tom had meant nothing to her. Now I had seen her sit beside Tom in a room where Gatsby could not reach her.

I walked away. Behind me, Gatsby kept watching the house. He had a signal to look for and a reason to wait. From what I had seen through the window, I doubted that the signal would ever come.

Vocabulary List

  • witnesses /ˈwɪtnəsəz/ — People who saw something happen.
  • grief /ɡrif/ — A very sad feeling after someone dies or is lost.
  • swerved /swɜrvd/ — Turned suddenly to one side while moving.
  • collision /kəˈlɪʒən/ — A crash when two things hit each other.
  • signal /ˈsɪɡnəl/ — A sign or light that gives a message.
  • protect /prəˈtɛkt/ — To keep someone safe from harm.
  • scene /sin/ — A place where something happens, or a view of it.
  • rushed /rʌʃt/ — Moved very quickly in a hurry.
  • struck /strʌk/ — Hit something with force.
  • edge /ɛdʒ/ — The outside part of something, where it ends.
  • understood /ˌʌndərˈstʊd/ — Knew what something meant or how someone felt.
