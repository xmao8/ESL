Negative Questions in English

Negative questions are questions that contain a negative word such as don't, doesn't, didn't, isn't, aren't, haven't, or can't. They are very common in everyday English.

Negative questions can have several purposes. They can be used to make suggestions, check information, express surprise, show expectations, or ask for agreement.

Two important patterns:
  • Why don't you + base verb? → usually a suggestion
  • Don't you + base verb? → usually confirmation or surprise

1) “Why don't you...?” — Making Suggestions

We commonly use Why don't you + base verb to suggest an action. Although the question contains don't, it usually means “I suggest that you...”

Why don't you + base verb...?
Examples:
  • Why don't you take a break?
  • Why don't you call your mom?
  • Why don't you try again?
  • Why don't you ask your teacher?
  • Why don't you take a taxi?

Why don't you take a taxi? usually means: I suggest that you take a taxi.

2) “Why don't we...?” — Making Suggestions Together

We use Why don't we + base verb when the speaker suggests doing something together.

Why don't we + base verb...?
Examples:
  • Why don't we go out for dinner?
  • Why don't we take a break?
  • Why don't we start tomorrow?
  • Why don't we ask John?
  • Why don't we watch a movie?

Why don't you...?

You should do it.

Why don't you call John?

Why don't we...?

You and I should do it.

Why don't we call John?

3) “Don't you...?” — Checking Information

Don't you + base verb is commonly used to check information. The speaker usually thinks the answer is probably yes.

Don't you + base verb...?
Examples:
  • Don't you like coffee?
  • Don't you work here?
  • Don't you remember him?
  • Don't you have a car?
  • Don't you know Sarah?

Don't you live in Los Angeles? means that the speaker thinks you probably live in Los Angeles and wants to confirm it.

4) “Don't you...?” — Expressing Surprise

Negative questions can also express surprise when the speaker expected a different answer.

Example:

A: I'm not coming to the party.

B: Don't you want to come?

Here, B is surprised because B expected A to want to come.

More examples:
  • Don't you like pizza?
  • Don't you want to come with us?
  • Don't you need any help?
  • Don't you remember her?

5) Positive Questions vs. Negative Questions

A positive question is usually more neutral. A negative question often shows that the speaker already has an expectation.

Positive Question Negative Question Difference
Do you like coffee? Don't you like coffee? The negative question suggests I expected you to like coffee.
Do you have a car? Don't you have a car? The negative question suggests I thought you had a car.
Are you tired? Aren't you tired? The negative question suggests I expected you to be tired.
Did you call Sarah? Didn't you call Sarah? The negative question suggests I thought you called Sarah.

6) “Didn't you...?” — Talking About the Past

We use Didn't you + base verb to check something that happened in the past.

Didn't you + base verb...?
Examples:
  • Didn't you call Sarah?
  • Didn't you see him yesterday?
  • Didn't you tell me about this?
  • Didn't you go to the party?
  • Didn't you meet John last week?

Didn't you meet John last week? means the speaker thought that you met John and wants to confirm it.

“Didn't you see the stop sign?” — Surprise, Frustration, or Criticism

The same Didn't + subject + base verb...? pattern is also used when the speaker expected the other person to have noticed, known, or done something — and is surprised, frustrated, or even criticizing them for not doing it.

Didn't + subject + base verb...?
Didn't + you + see...?
Example:

A: Why did you drive through the intersection?

B: I didn't see the sign.

A: Didn't you see the stop sign?

Here, A is not simply asking for information. A is implying:

  • “I thought you saw the stop sign.”
  • “You should have seen the stop sign.”
Question Meaning
Did you see the stop sign? Neutral question — I don't know whether you saw it.
Didn't you see the stop sign? I expected you to see it / I'm surprised you didn't see it.
ESL Tip: Be careful with tone. “Didn't you see the stop sign?” can sound like a simple check, but depending on your voice and the situation, it can also sound like blame — as if you're saying the other person made an obvious mistake.

7) “Aren't you...?” — Expectations and Surprise

With the verb be, we use Aren't you...? Negative questions with be often express an expectation or surprise.

Aren't you + adjective/noun...?
Examples:
  • Aren't you tired?
  • Aren't you hungry?
  • Aren't you ready?
  • Aren't you a teacher?
  • Aren't you coming with us?

8) “Haven't you...?” — Something Expected to Have Happened

We use Haven't you + past participle when we expected something to have happened already.

Haven't you + past participle...?
Examples:
  • Haven't you finished?
  • Haven't you seen this movie?
  • Haven't you met her before?
  • Haven't you done your homework?

Haven't you finished your homework? suggests that the speaker expected the homework to be finished already.

9) Other Common Negative Questions

Pattern Example Common Purpose
Why don't you...? Why don't you sit down? Suggestion
Why don't we...? Why don't we order pizza? Suggestion together
Don't you...? Don't you know him? Confirmation / surprise
Didn't you...? Didn't you call her? Past confirmation
Didn't you...? Didn't you see the stop sign? Surprise / frustration / criticism
Aren't you...? Aren't you hungry? Expectation / surprise
Isn't it...? Isn't it beautiful? Agreement / confirmation
Haven't you...? Haven't you finished? Expectation
Can't you...? Can't you help me? Request / complaint
Won't you...? Won't you come in? Invitation / request

10) A Useful Comparison

Why don't you...?

→ Usually a suggestion

Why don't you get some rest?

= I think you should get some rest.

Don't you...?

→ Usually confirmation or surprise

Don't you want to get some rest?

= I thought you wanted to get some rest.

ESL Tip: The two forms look similar, but they have different purposes. “Why don't you...?” usually gives a suggestion, while “Don't you...?” usually checks information or expresses surprise.

11) Quick Practice

Choose the best negative question for each situation.

  1. Your friend is very tired. You suggest going home early.
    Why don't you go home early?
  2. You thought your friend worked at a hospital.
    Don't you work at the hospital?
  3. You want to suggest having dinner together.
    Why don't we have dinner together?
  4. You thought Sarah had already finished her homework.
    Haven't you finished your homework?
  5. You are surprised that your friend doesn't want pizza.
    Don't you like pizza?
  6. Someone drove through an intersection and you thought they clearly saw the sign.
    Didn't you see the stop sign?

Key Takeaway

  • Why don't you...? → Make a suggestion
  • Why don't we...? → Suggest doing something together
  • Don't you...? → Check information or express surprise
  • Didn't you...? → Check something about the past
  • Didn't you...? (e.g. Didn't you see the stop sign?) → Show surprise, frustration, or criticism when something expected didn't happen
  • Aren't you...? → Express an expectation or surprise
  • Haven't you...? → Ask about something expected to have happened
