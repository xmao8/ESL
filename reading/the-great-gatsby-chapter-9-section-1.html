The Great Gatsby — Chapter IX, Section 1: Looking for Gatsby’s Friends (Abridged Reader)

For days after Gatsby’s death, his house was full of people again. This time they were police officers, photographers, and reporters. Even children came to look at the pool. Newspapers printed stories about Gatsby and Wilson that would have been hard to recognize if I had not known their names. At the official inquiry, Myrtle’s sister Catherine said that Myrtle had never known Gatsby and that she had been happy with her husband. Whatever Catherine knew, she kept to herself. The public heard a simpler story than the one I had seen.

I telephoned Daisy soon after we found Gatsby. The servant told me that she and Tom had left with their luggage. They had given no address and had not said when they would return. I had called without even asking myself what I expected Daisy to do. Now I put down the telephone and tried to think of someone else. Gatsby’s house had once been so crowded that I could hardly find him. Now it seemed that only I cared what happened to him next.

I sent a letter to Meyer Wolfshiem. He wrote back that the news had shocked him, but he was busy and did not want to get involved. When the telephone rang and the operator said Chicago was calling, I thought for an instant that it might be Daisy. Instead, a man whose name I did not recognize started talking about someone who had been caught trying to pass some bonds. When I told him Gatsby was dead, the other end of the line went silent. Then the man hung up. Whoever he was, he was not a friend I could ask to come out to West Egg.

A telegram arrived from Minnesota, signed Henry C. Gatz. The sender asked me to postpone the funeral until he arrived. He was Gatsby’s father. He had read the news in a Chicago newspaper and taken a train east. When he reached the house he was shaking. I tried to give him something to drink, but the glass slipped in his hand. He asked me where his son was. He called him Jimmy. I showed him into the room and left him there alone.

When Mr. Gatz came out, he looked properly at the house for the first time. From the height of the hall and the size of the rooms, he could tell the sort of position Jimmy had reached. His grief did not disappear, but pride was mixed with it. I offered to make arrangements to take his son west. Mr. Gatz said that Jimmy had always liked it better in the East and should be buried there. He asked whether we had been friends. I told him we had been close.

That night a man called and asked me who I was before saying his own name. It was Klipspringer, who had spent so much time at Gatsby’s house that he had practically lived there. I told him when the funeral would be. He was staying with friends in Greenwich and said they expected him to go to a picnic. He would try to come, he said, but there was something else he wanted to ask. He had left a pair of tennis shoes at the house. Could the butler send them on?

I hung up before he finished giving me the address. Earlier that evening, I had hoped there might be one more person standing beside Mr. Gatz at the grave. I had not thought a pair of shoes would matter more than a friend. Yet for Klipspringer, it appeared, the important thing about Gatsby’s house was something he could have sent to another address.

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

Vocabulary List

  • reporters /rɪˈpɔrtərz/ — People who write news stories for newspapers.
  • inquiry /ɪnˈkwaɪri/ — An official look into what happened.
  • luggage /ˈlʌɡɪdʒ/ — Bags that you take with you when you travel.
  • involved /ɪnˈvɑlvd/ — Taking part in something.
  • postpone /poʊstˈpoʊn/ — To move something to a later time.
  • arrangements /əˈreɪndʒmənts/ — Plans made so that something can happen.
  • practically /ˈpræktɪkli/ — Almost, or in almost every way.
  • telegram /ˈtɛləˌɡræm/ — A message sent quickly by wire long ago.
  • buried /ˈbɛrid/ — Put into the ground after death.
  • recognize /ˈrɛkəɡˌnaɪz/ — To know who someone is because you have seen them before.
  • properly /ˈprɑpərli/ — In the right and correct way.
