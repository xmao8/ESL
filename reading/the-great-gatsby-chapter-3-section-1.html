The Great Gatsby — Chapter III, Section 1: An Invitation to Gatsby's House (Abridged Reader)

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

On summer nights there was music at my neighbor's house. From my yard I could see people moving through his garden among the lights. In the afternoon, guests swam from his beach or lay in the sun. On weekends his large car carried them back and forth from the city. Then on Mondays servants worked all day repairing what the night before had left behind. The festival next door appeared almost by magic, if you only looked at it on Saturday.

On Fridays boxes of oranges and lemons arrived from New York. By Monday the fruit had become a pile of empty halves. There was a machine in the kitchen that could squeeze two hundred oranges in half an hour, provided a servant pressed the button two hundred times. On party nights, a whole orchestra arrived. Colored lights shone over tables loaded with food. The louder the music grew, the less anyone seemed to mind that they hardly knew the people beside them.

I was one of the few guests who actually received an invitation. Gatsby's driver crossed my lawn one Saturday morning with a note. At the party that evening, I tried to find Gatsby so I could thank him. The people I asked were surprised, as if knowing the host had nothing to do with coming to his house. Some guests had simply heard about the party and arrived without an introduction. I wandered from one group to another until I saw a familiar face: Jordan Baker.

She greeted me calmly, which was exactly what I needed. We sat down with two women in yellow dresses. One of them said she had torn a dress at a previous party, and Gatsby had sent her a new one. It was a generous gesture, but it did not make him seem easier to understand. Someone suggested that Gatsby had once killed a man. Someone else said he had been a German spy during the war. Another guest said that made no sense because he had served in the American army. Nobody could prove anything. The less they knew, the more they had to say.

Jordan took me to a table where her own friends from East Egg were sitting. They behaved very correctly and seemed determined not to be impressed by anything. After a little while Jordan whispered that the company was much too polite for her. We got up to look for Gatsby. We did not find him at the bar or on the porch, so we tried an important-looking door. It led to a library with walls of dark, carved wood.

A middle-aged man in huge round glasses sat beside the bookshelves. I came to think of him as Owl Eyes. He was excited to tell us that the books were real. He had expected to find pieces of cardboard on the shelves. Instead, he pulled a volume off the shelf and showed us printed pages. The pages had not been cut apart, however, as a reader would have had to do to open an old book all the way. Apparently the owner had bought real books without bothering to read this one.

The man quickly put the book back, as though taking it away might make the room fall apart. He told us a woman had brought him to the party. He had been drinking for about a week and thought sitting in a library might help him become sober. He had not made up his mind about that. He had certainly made up his mind about the books. We shook hands with him and went back out to the garden.

Vocabulary List

  • invitation /ˌɪnvɪˈteɪʃən/ — A written or spoken request to come to something.
  • generous /ˈdʒɛnərəs/ — Willing to give a lot to other people.
  • gesture /ˈdʒɛstʃɚ/ — An action that shows a feeling or an idea.
  • determined /dɪˈtɝmɪnd/ — Having decided firmly to do something.
  • carved /kɑrvd/ — Cut into wood or stone to make a shape.
  • volume /ˈvɑljəm/ — One book, often part of a set.
  • sober /ˈsoʊbɚ/ — Not drunk.
  • repairing /rɪˈpɛrɪŋ/ — Fixing things that are broken or damaged.
  • orchestra /ˈɔrkɪstrə/ — A large group of people who play music together.
  • squeeze /skwiz/ — To press something hard to get liquid out of it.
  • familiar /fəˈmɪliɚ/ — Known well because you have seen it before.
  • wandered /ˈwɑndɚd/ — Walked slowly with no clear plan.
