The American Yawp — Chapter Twenty-Five: The Sixties
Abridged for Intermediate-High Learners

The 1960s are remembered as a decade of hope and protest, when the civil rights movement and calls for peace made many Americans believe in a more inclusive nation. But the decade also brought tragedy and division: the Vietnam War, urban riots, political assassinations, and a conservative backlash against change.

The civil rights movement supplied much of the decade’s energy. In 1960, four Black students sat down at a whites-only lunch counter in Greensboro, North Carolina, and refused to leave until served. Their sit-in inspired protests across the South, and young activists formed the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC) to organize marches, sit-ins, and boycott campaigns.

In 1961, the Congress of Racial Equality organized Freedom Rides against segregation on interstate buses. Riders were beaten by mobs in Alabama, but the government finally enforced integrated travel. In 1963, Martin Luther King Jr. led the Birmingham Campaign in America’s most segregated city, where police chief Bull Connor turned fire hoses and attack dogs on peaceful marchers and a church bombing killed four Black girls. The shocking violence pressured the nation to act.

Alabama governor George Wallace became the face of white resistance, declaring “segregation now, segregation tomorrow, segregation forever.” In June 1963 he blocked Black students from entering the University of Alabama. That August, a quarter of a million people joined the March on Washington, where King delivered his “I Have a Dream” speech. Three months later, an assassin killed Kennedy in Dallas, and Vice President Lyndon Johnson took office.

Johnson pushed Kennedy’s stalled civil rights bill through Congress, signing the Civil Rights Act in 1964, which banned segregation in public places and outlawed discrimination in jobs. During 1964’s Freedom Summer, activists registered Black voters in Mississippi, where three young workers were murdered by Klansmen. After marchers were beaten by police on “Bloody Sunday” in Selma, Alabama, Congress passed landmark legislation — the Voting Rights Act of 1965 — protecting Black voting rights.

Johnson also launched the Great Society, a sweeping plan to end poverty and racial inequality. It created Medicare and Medicaid, expanded aid to schools, and funded the arts; the Immigration Act of 1965 ended quotas favoring northern Europeans. But the War on Poverty faced criticism, and days after the Voting Rights Act passed, riots erupted in Watts, Los Angeles. More riots followed in Newark and Detroit in 1967, as “white flight” to the suburbs left cities poorer and more segregated.

American involvement in Vietnam grew from the fear that communism would spread through Asia — the Domino Theory. After France’s defeat in 1954, the U.S. backed South Vietnam against the communist North. A 1964 clash in the Gulf of Tonkin let Johnson send combat troops; by 1968, half a million Americans were fighting a communist insurgency measured in body counts. Nearly 20,000 had died, victory was nowhere in sight, and protests spread.

The decade also transformed American culture. Young people embraced the counterculture — rock music, casual fashion like the miniskirt, and open rebellion against old rules. Frustrated by slow progress, many Black activists embraced the more militant message of Black Power: Malcolm X urged freedom “by any means necessary” before his 1965 assassination, and the Black Panther Party organized armed self-defense and community programs in Oakland. Other movements followed: Native Americans occupied Alcatraz Island, Cesar Chavez led farm workers in California, Betty Friedan’s 1963 book sparked the modern feminist movement, and Rachel Carson’s Silent Spring (1962), which warned that each pesticide threatened human health, launched the environmental movement.

In 1969, Americans celebrated the moon landing, fulfilling Kennedy’s 1961 promise. Yet the achievement only briefly interrupted years of turmoil. The Vietnam War disillusioned a generation, and assassins killed the nation’s leaders. The forward-looking spirit of the sixties had faded, and uncertainty loomed.

Vocabulary List

  • segregation — the practice of keeping people of different races apart.
  • sit-in — a protest in which people sit down in a place and refuse to leave.
  • boycott — a protest in which people refuse to buy or use something.
  • discrimination — unfair treatment of people because of their race, sex, or background.
  • legislation — laws made by a government.
  • backlash — a strong negative reaction against a change or idea.
  • assassin — a person who murders an important public figure.
  • poverty — the condition of being very poor.
  • insurgency — an armed rebellion against a government.
  • counterculture — a movement that rejects the values of mainstream society.
  • rebellion — open resistance against rules or authority.
  • militant — aggressive and forceful in supporting a cause.
  • feminist — supporting equal rights for women.
  • pesticide — a chemical used to kill insects that damage crops.
  • inequality — an unfair difference between groups of people.
