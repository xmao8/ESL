The Great Gatsby — Chapter II, Section 3: A Party Turns Violent (Abridged Reader)

The talk turned to people's marriages. Mrs. McKee said she had almost married someone her friends thought was beneath her. Myrtle pointed out that at least she had stopped in time. When Catherine asked why Myrtle had married George, Myrtle said she had thought he was a gentleman. What mattered to her now was a story about his wedding suit. George had borrowed it. Myrtle said she had not known that until after the wedding, when the owner came to ask for it back.

Catherine remembered things differently. She thought her sister had been quite fond of George when they married. Myrtle denied it. She pointed at me for comparison and said that she had never cared any more for George than she had for me. Everyone looked at me as though I had been accused of something, though I had not known Myrtle until that afternoon. I preferred not to be part of that particular story.

I thought about leaving. Several times I planned to walk out and go east through the park. But each time I started to go, someone spoke to me, or a fresh argument broke out nearby. Now and then I looked at our windows from inside and imagined a stranger in the street looking up at them. The stranger would see yellow light and have no idea what was happening behind it. I felt like that stranger and one of the people in the room at the same time. I wanted to know more and wanted to get away.

Myrtle moved her chair close to mine to tell me how she had met Tom. She had been riding a train to New York to visit Catherine. Tom sat across from her. His fine suit and polished shoes caught her attention, but she pretended to be looking at the advertisement over his head. When they reached the city, she got into a taxi with him. She told me she had kept thinking that she could not live forever. Whatever pleasure she could get, she had decided, she had better take while she could.

Then Myrtle turned away and offered to give Mrs. McKee her dress as soon as she was finished with it. She began a list of things she wanted, including a collar for the dog and a wreath for her mother's grave. Time seemed to jump forward whenever I looked at my watch. Mr. McKee fell asleep in a chair. The puppy sat on the table. People came and went without purpose.

Sometime near midnight, Tom told Myrtle she had no right to say Daisy's name. Myrtle challenged him by shouting it again and again. He struck her with an open hand and broke her nose. There was blood on the towels, and the women hurried to help her. The man who had bought a puppy a few hours earlier was the same man who had just hit her. It had all happened so quickly that the room seemed to take a moment to understand it.

Mr. McKee woke up and went toward the door. I took my hat and followed him into the elevator. He invited me to lunch. I said yes without settling where or when. My memory breaks into pieces after that. For a moment I remember Mr. McKee sitting on his bed with a collection of photographs. Then I was at Pennsylvania Station, half asleep in the cold, waiting for the four o'clock train. I looked down at the morning newspaper. After all that talk, I was glad to be somewhere no one asked me to speak.

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

Vocabulary List

  • beneath /bɪˈniθ/ — Below someone in rank or worth.
  • gentleman /ˈdʒɛntəlmən/ — A man with good manners who treats people well.
  • borrowed /ˈbɑroʊd/ — Took something to use for a short time and then return it.
  • denied /dɪˈnaɪd/ — Said something was not true.
  • comparison /kəmˈpærɪsən/ — Looking at two people or things to see how they are alike or different.
  • accused /əˈkjuzd/ — Said that someone did something wrong.
  • particular /pɚˈtɪkjəlɚ/ — One special thing, not a general one.
  • polished /ˈpɑlɪʃt/ — Shiny and clean from rubbing.
  • pleasure /ˈplɛʒɚ/ — A feeling of happiness and enjoyment.
  • wreath /riθ/ — A circle of flowers or leaves, often put on a grave.
  • challenged /ˈtʃælɪndʒd/ — Dared someone by doing the thing they said not to do.
  • elevator /ˈɛləveɪtɚ/ — A small room that carries people up and down in a building.
