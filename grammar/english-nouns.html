English Nouns

A noun is a word for a person, a place, a thing, or an idea. Every sentence has a noun — or a pronoun doing the work of a noun. Nouns come in different kinds, and each kind works in its own way.

The same word can be a noun in one sentence and a verb in another. The kind of word depends on how it is used: My work is difficult (noun) but I work every day (verb).

1. Common Nouns and Proper Nouns

A common noun names a general person, place, or thing: teacher, city, book, dog. It is not capitalized unless it starts a sentence. A proper noun names one specific person, place, or thing: Maria, Los Angeles, the Amazon River. It always starts with a capital letter.

  • She is a teacher. (common)
  • Maria is my teacher. (proper)
  • They live in a city. (common)
  • They live in Los Angeles. (proper)

Days, months, and languages are proper nouns too: Monday, November, English.

2. Countable Nouns and Uncountable Nouns

A countable noun is something you can count: one book, two books. It has a singular and a plural form, and you can use it with a / an, many, and few. An uncountable noun is something you cannot count: water, money, information, advice. It has no plural form, and you cannot use it with a / an. Use some, much, and little with it instead.

  • I have three books. (countable)
  • I need some water. (uncountable)
  • She has many friends. (countable)
  • He gave me good advice. (uncountable)

Some nouns are both, with a change in meaning: hair is uncountable when you mean all the hair on your head, but a hair is one single hair. Paper is the material, but a paper is a newspaper or an essay.

To count an uncountable noun, use a unit word: a piece of advice, two pieces of information, three bottles of water. A noun can also change meaning: I ate chicken (the food) but They keep three chickens (the animals); Would you like some coffee? (the drink) but We ordered two coffees (two servings).

For the full picture, with more examples and a list of common uncountable nouns, see Countable vs Noncountable Nouns. For the words that go with them, see Quantifiers & Determiners and English Articles: A, An, and The.

3. Singular and Plural Nouns

Most nouns make the plural by adding -s: book — books. A few spelling rules change the ending:

  • Add -es after -s, -x, -ch, -sh, and -z: box — boxes, watch — watches, quiz — quizzes.
  • Nouns ending in -o vary: tomato — tomatoes, but photo — photos.
  • Some nouns ending in -f or -fe just add -s: roof — roofs, belief — beliefs.
  • Change -y to -ies after a consonant: city — cities. After a vowel, just add -s: boy — boys.
  • Change -f or -fe to -ves: leaf — leaves, wife — wives.

Some plurals are irregular — you have to learn them: man — men, woman — women, child — children, foot — feet, tooth — teeth, person — people, mouse — mice.

Do not use an apostrophe to make an ordinary plural: three books, not three book’s.

  • Some nouns have the same form in the singular and the plural: sheep, fish, deer, series, species — one sheep, ten sheep.
  • Some nouns are always plural, and we use a pair of with them: glasses, pants, scissors, jeans — a pair of glasses.
A few nouns look plural but are singular: The news is good. Mathematics is hard. And people is always plural: People are waiting outside. The main noun controls the verb: The box of pencils is on the desk — the main noun is box, not pencils. With a pair, the verb is singular: A pair of scissors is on the table. See Subject-Verb Agreement.

4. Possessive Nouns

A possessive noun shows who owns something. Add 's to a singular noun, and ' to a plural noun that already ends in -s.

  • Maria's book is on the table. (singular)
  • The teachers' room is on the second floor. (plural ending in -s)
  • The children's toys are in the box. (irregular plural — add 's)
  • Maria and Luis's house is near the park. (one house, shared by two people)
  • Maria's and Luis's houses are on the same street. (two houses, one each)

For things, we usually use of instead of 's: the name of the street, the top of the mountain.

Compare the three forms: students (more than one student), student's (belonging to one student), and students' (belonging to several students).

Its is possessive: The dog wagged its tail. But it's means it is or it has: It's raining.
Do not confuse possessive nouns with possessive adjectives: Maria's book (possessive noun) means the same as her book (possessive adjective). See Possessive Adjectives vs. Pronouns.

5. Compound Nouns

A compound noun is made of two words joined together: toothbrush, classroom, notebook, bus stop, swimming pool, mother-in-law. Some are written as one word, some as two words, and some with a hyphen — there is no simple rule, so learn them as you meet them.

  • She bought a new toothbrush.
  • We waited at the bus stop.
  • His mother-in-law visits every Sunday.

The first word works like an adjective, and it stays singular: a shoe store (not a shoes store). The plural goes on the main noun: two toothbrushes, three bus stops, two mothers-in-law.

6. Collective Nouns

A collective noun is one word for a whole group: team, family, class, group, crowd, audience, committee. In American English it usually takes a singular verb.

  • The team is winning.
  • My family is very large.
  • The audience was quiet during the show.

When the members of the group act as separate people, a plural verb is possible: The team are arguing with each other. In American English it is more common to say The team members are arguing with each other. In British English, the plural verb is also common when the members are the focus.

7. Concrete Nouns and Abstract Nouns

A concrete noun is something you can see or touch: book, dog, rain, coffee. An abstract noun is an idea, a feeling, or a quality: love, happiness, freedom, information, advice.

  • The dog is sleeping. (concrete)
  • Happiness is more important than money. (abstract)
  • She gave me good advice. (abstract)

Most abstract nouns are uncountable: happiness, advice, information, freedom.

These kinds overlap: team is both common and collective, and happiness is both common and abstract. One noun can belong to more than one kind at the same time.

8. Nouns at a Glance

A noun often works with other words in a noun phrase — a noun with words that describe or identify it. In the three new students, the main noun is students. A noun can do many jobs in a sentence:

  • Subject: The teacher smiled.
  • Object: She bought a book.
  • Indirect object: He gave Maria a pen.
  • Subject complement: My sister is a teacher.
  • After a preposition: in the classroom, on the bus.
  • Describing another noun: a bus driver, a coffee cup.
  • After a determiner: this book, my teacher, three dogs.

A noun can also follow an adjective: a beautiful day, an interesting story. For how adjectives compare nouns, see Comparatives & Superlatives with Nouns.

9. Articles and Other Determiners

A singular countable common noun normally needs a determiner — a word like a, an, the, my, or this that goes before it.

  • Use a or an for one thing that is not specific: I need a pen. She ate an apple.
  • Use the for something both people can identify: Please close the door.
  • Use my, your, this, or another determiner: My book is here. This chair is comfortable.
  • Use no article for plural nouns or uncountable nouns used in general: Dogs need exercise. Water is essential.
  • Use the for a specific plural or uncountable noun: The dogs next door are noisy. The water in this glass is cold.

Choose a or an by sound, not by letter: a university (the u sounds like y) but an hour (the h is silent). For the full lesson, see English Articles: A, An, and The.

Common Mistakes

  • Adding -s to an uncountable noun: Information, advice, and furniture have no plural form.
    Incorrect: I need some informations. / I need an advice. / These informations are useful. Correct: I need some information. / I need some advice. / This information is useful.
  • Missing determiner: A singular countable noun needs a determiner like a, an, or the.
    Incorrect: She is teacher. Correct: She is a teacher.
  • Forgetting the capital letter on a proper noun: Names of people, places, days, months, and languages always start with a capital letter.
    Incorrect: I live in los angeles. / I study english. Correct: I live in Los Angeles. / I study English.
  • Making the first word of a compound noun plural: In a compound noun, only the last word takes the plural.
    Incorrect: a shoes store Correct: a shoe store — two shoe stores
  • Wrong irregular plural: Some plurals change the word itself — do not just add -s.
    Incorrect: I have two childs. Correct: I have two children.
  • Wrong possessive: Irregular plurals take 's, and plural nouns ending in -s take only '.
    Incorrect: the childs toys / the teachers room / the students's books Correct: the children's toys / the teachers' room / the students' books
  • “People is” / “The news are”: People is always plural, and news is always singular.
    Incorrect: People is waiting. / The news are good. Correct: People are waiting. / The news is good.

Summary

  • Common nouns name general things (teacher, city); proper nouns name one specific thing and start with a capital letter (Maria, Los Angeles).
  • Countable nouns have a singular and a plural (one book, two books); uncountable nouns have no plural (water, information, advice). Count an uncountable noun with a unit: a piece of advice, three bottles of water.
  • Plural nouns usually add -s, with spelling changes like city — cities and leaf — leaves; -o endings vary (tomato — tomatoes, photo — photos); some are irregular (child — children, person — people), and some never change (sheep, fish). Never use an apostrophe for an ordinary plural (three books, not three book’s).
  • Possessive nouns show ownership: Maria's book, the teachers' room, the children's toys. Its is possessive (its tail); it's means it is.
  • Compound nouns join two words (toothbrush, bus stop) — the first word stays singular, the plural goes on the last word.
  • Collective nouns (team, family, audience) name a whole group and usually take a singular verb.
  • Concrete nouns can be seen or touched (dog, rain); abstract nouns are ideas and feelings (love, happiness), and most are uncountable.
  • These kinds overlap: team is both common and collective; happiness is both common and abstract.
  • A singular countable noun usually needs a determiner (a pen, the door, my book); use no article for plural or uncountable nouns used in general (Dogs need exercise. Water is essential.). Choose a or an by sound: a university, an hour.
  • The main noun controls the verb: The box of pencils is on the desk.
  • A noun can be the subject, the object, the indirect object (He gave Maria a pen), the subject complement (My sister is a teacher), or the object of a preposition; it can follow a determiner or describe another noun. Nouns often work in a noun phrase (the three new students).

Practice by sorting the nouns in a short paragraph: which ones are common and which ones are proper? Which ones are countable and which ones are not? Then try making one plural and one possessive from each noun.
