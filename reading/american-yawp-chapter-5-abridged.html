The American Yawp — Chapter Five: Colonial Society
Abridged for Intermediate-High Learners

In the 1700s, the thirteen British colonies in North America grew richer and more connected to Britain through trade. At the same time, a new and distinctly American culture began to form. Immigrants from many European nations lived alongside Indigenous Americans and enslaved Africans. Colonial life was shaped by British ways, but it was slowly becoming something different.

This was the age of the "consumer revolution." Better manufacturing and easy credit let ordinary colonists buy fine clothes, furniture, tea sets, and books. Spending money became a sign of respectability. Many colonists went into debt to buy British-made products.

Money itself was a problem. The colonies had few gold or silver coins, so people used barter, nails, and wampum from Indigenous nations. In Virginia, tobacco became money. In 1690, Massachusetts became the first place in the Western Hemisphere to print paper bills. But paper money lost value quickly, so Britain restricted it with the Currency Acts of 1751 and 1763.

The Caribbean sugar islands, such as Barbados and Jamaica, were more valuable to Britain than the mainland colonies. After 1764, new taxes on sugar, paper, lead, glass, and tea angered colonists. Merchants organized a boycott of British goods, and women such as the Daughters of Liberty wove homespun cloth as a political statement.

By 1775, one in twenty colonists lived in cities. Philadelphia, with about 40,000 people, was the largest, followed by New York, Boston, and Charleston. City life was highly unequal: laborers, apprentices, and enslaved people at the bottom, wealthy merchants at the top.

Slavery was legal in every colony by 1750, but it looked different in each region. Virginia's economy depended on tobacco grown by enslaved Africans under the gang system. South Carolina's rice plantations used the task system, which gave enslaved people time to grow their own food. They kept many African traditions, creating the Gullah and Geechee culture. In 1739, about eighty enslaved people rose up in the Stono Rebellion, marching toward freedom in Spanish Florida before they were defeated.

Resistance to slavery grew. Quakers were the first group to oppose it, and by 1772 slave-owning Quakers could be expelled. In New England, slavery was legal but small: Massachusetts had only about 2 percent enslaved people in the 1760s. Still, a New England merchant could profit greatly from the slave trade itself.

Colonial politics also differed from Britain's. More white men could vote, and an elected assembly in each colony held real power over taxes and budgets. Colonies had three forms of government: provincial colonies with a royal governor, proprietary colonies, and charter colonies that elected their own governors. Many colonists believed in the social contract — the idea that government came from the people.

Indigenous nations remained powerful. The Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) League united six nations in one of the world's oldest participatory democracies. In 1754, Benjamin Franklin admired their system when he proposed a plan of union at the Albany Congress. In the Southwest, the Comanche limited Spanish expansion and controlled trade on the frontier.

The Seven Years' War (1754–1763) changed everything. It began when young George Washington's forces killed a French diplomat in 1754. Britain won, taking Canada from France in the 1763 Treaty of Paris, but the war cost over £140 million. To pay this debt, Britain taxed the colonies more heavily.

Victory also brought conflict with Indigenous nations. The Ottawa leader Pontiac, inspired by the prophet Neolin, led Pontiac's War (1763–1766) against British forts. Though the uprising failed, it frightened Britain into issuing the Royal Proclamation of 1763, which banned colonial settlement west of the Appalachian Mountains. Colonists who wanted that land were furious. The war had brought the colonies together, but British taxes and limits on settlement were pulling them apart.

Vocabulary List

  • consumer — a person who buys goods and services.
  • revolution — a sudden, complete change.
  • currency — the money used in a country.
  • colony — a territory controlled by a distant country.
  • slavery — the system of owning people as property.
  • freedom — the state of being free; liberty.
  • assembly — a group of elected people who make laws.
  • governor — the leader of a colony or state.
  • charter — an official document giving rights to a colony.
  • rebellion — a fight against those in power.
  • treaty — a formal agreement between nations.
  • frontier — the edge of settled land, near wild areas.
  • trade — the buying and selling of goods.
  • merchant — a person who buys and sells goods for profit.
  • boycott — a refusal to buy goods as a protest.

Discussion Questions

  1. In the 1700s, colonists boycotted British goods and wore homespun cloth to make a political statement. Do you think buying certain products can be a political statement today? Why or why not?
  2. The text says colonists began to see themselves as "Americans, not Britons." What things make people today feel that they belong to one nation?
