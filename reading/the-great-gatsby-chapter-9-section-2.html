The Great Gatsby — Chapter IX, Section 2: A Small Funeral (Abridged Reader)

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

On the morning of the funeral I went into New York to see Wolfshiem in person. A woman at his office told me that he was in Chicago. Almost at once I heard his voice behind an inside door. When I mentioned Gatsby, the woman went away, and Wolfshiem himself came out. He spoke sadly and offered me a cigar. I hoped that speaking to him in person might change his mind.

Wolfshiem remembered a young man just out of the army. Gatsby still wore his uniform, he said, because he could not afford other clothes. When Gatsby asked for work, Wolfshiem took him to lunch and saw how hungry he was. He spoke with pride about recognizing Gatsby’s abilities and bringing him into business. He said they had been close and had done everything together. Then I asked him to come with me that afternoon. His eyes filled with tears, but he still refused. He said he made a rule not to get involved when a man had been killed.

The sky darkened while I was in the city, and a light rain was falling when I reached West Egg. Mr. Gatz was walking up and down the hall. He took out a worn photograph of Gatsby’s house. Jimmy had sent it to him. The photograph was cracked at the corners because he had shown it to so many people. He pointed out the details to me with as much care as if we could not see the actual house around us. He also told me that his son had bought him a house of his own.

Then Mr. Gatz showed me an old book called Hopalong Cassidy. Gatsby had used the last page to write a daily schedule in 1906. He meant to get up at six, exercise, work, and study. He had set aside an hour to practice speaking well and carrying himself with confidence. He also listed a few promises to himself: he would read a useful book or magazine every week, save money, and be better to his parents. Mr. Gatz read the list out as if it explained everything about the man his son had become. I could see on that page how early Jimmy had begun planning a different life.

The minister arrived shortly before three. Mr. Gatz and I kept looking out the windows for cars. The servants stood in the hall, waiting. I asked the minister to wait another half hour, but nobody else came to the house. In the end we left for the cemetery. Gatsby’s father, the minister, and I rode together. Some servants and the West Egg postman followed in Gatsby’s station wagon. The rain was still coming down when we reached the gate.

Just then a man got out of another car and hurried after us. I recognized the thick glasses of Owl Eyes, the man I had met in Gatsby’s library during one of the parties. I did not know his real name or how he had heard about the funeral. But there he was, wiping the rain off his glasses so he could see. He had been unable to get to the house, he said afterward. He was astonished that no one else had made it there when people used to turn up by the hundreds.

As we stood beside the grave, I remembered something I had been trying not to think about. Daisy had sent neither a message nor a flower. There was a time when Gatsby might have thought he could bring almost anyone to his house simply by turning on the lights. At his grave, a man whose name I did not know stood in the rain with us.

Vocabulary List

  • afford /əˈfɔrd/ — To have enough money to buy or do something.
  • abilities /əˈbɪlətiz/ — Things that a person is able to do well.
  • refused /rɪˈfjuzd/ — Said no to something that was asked.
  • worn /wɔrn/ — Old and damaged from a lot of use.
  • cracked /krækt/ — Broken with a thin line, but not in pieces.
  • schedule /ˈskɛdʒul/ — A plan that shows when to do things each day.
  • astonished /əˈstɑnɪʃt/ — Very surprised by something unexpected.
  • cemetery /ˈsɛməˌtɛri/ — A place where dead people are buried.
  • minister /ˈmɪnəstər/ — A person who leads a church service.
  • uniform /ˈjunəˌfɔrm/ — Special clothes worn by people in one job.
  • sadly /ˈsædli/ — In a way that shows sadness.
