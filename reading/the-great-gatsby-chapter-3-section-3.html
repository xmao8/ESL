The Great Gatsby — Chapter III, Section 3: After the Party (Abridged Reader)

I apologized to Gatsby for not recognizing him earlier. He told me not to worry and reminded me about the hydroplane at nine in the morning. A servant came with a call from Philadelphia, but Gatsby waited long enough to smile and say good night. Somehow his smile made me feel that he had wanted me to be among the last guests to leave.

Outside, headlights shone on a new car in a ditch. It had hit a wall and lost a wheel. Several drivers had gathered around it, and their cars blocked the road. People farther back sounded their horns. I recognized Owl Eyes standing next to the wreck. The crowd assumed he had been driving. He explained that he knew little about cars and had not been driving. The door opened and another man, the actual driver, slowly stepped out. He thought they had run out of gas.

Everyone pointed to the wheel lying on the ground, but the man still wanted to back the car out. By then I had heard enough and turned toward home. When I looked back, the garden was still glowing and Gatsby stood alone on the porch, raising one hand in farewell. Seen from the road, the great house suddenly seemed to have an extraordinary amount of empty space inside it.

Reading over this story, I see that I have made my summer sound like a long series of dinners and parties. In fact, most days I worked at Probity Trust in New York. I ate lunch with the other young men at the firm, and after dinner I often studied investments in the library at the Yale Club. If the evening was pleasant, I walked to Pennsylvania Station to catch the train. I liked the city at that hour. There was always something to look at, and I could imagine becoming part of a different life on every block. Then I would see a lonely man waiting in front of a restaurant window and recognize more of myself in him than I wanted to.

I lost touch with Jordan for a while and saw her again in the middle of the summer. At first I was flattered to go places with a famous golfer. Later I wanted to know what lay behind the calm expression she wore when she met people. One day she left a borrowed car out in the rain with its top down and lied about it. That reminded me of a rumor from her first important golf tournament. She had supposedly moved her ball from a bad position. One witness took back his statement and another said he might have been mistaken. The story had never been proved, but I found it easier to believe after the rain.

On another drive, Jordan passed some workers so closely that she nearly hit one. I told her she should be more careful. She said other people were careful and would move out of her way. I asked what would happen if she met a driver just as careless as she was. She said she hated careless people, and that was why she liked me.

For a moment I thought I loved her. But I was still writing weekly letters to a woman back home and signing them with love. We were not formally engaged, yet something had been understood between us. I knew I ought to put an end to that understanding before I made a promise to someone else. I took pride in being honest. In fact, I considered myself one of the few honest people I had ever known.

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

Vocabulary List

  • farewell /fɛrˈwɛl/ — A goodbye.
  • flattered /ˈflætɚd/ — Pleased because someone gave you special attention.
  • witness /ˈwɪtnəs/ — A person who saw something happen.
  • statement /ˈsteɪtmənt/ — Something a person says or writes officially.
  • mistaken /mɪˈsteɪkən/ — Wrong about something.
  • careless /ˈkɛrləs/ — Not giving enough care or attention.
  • formally /ˈfɔrməli/ — In an official or proper way.
  • understanding /ˌʌndɚˈstændɪŋ/ — An agreement that people have without writing it down.
  • honest /ˈɑnɪst/ — Telling the truth and not cheating.
  • pride /praɪd/ — A good feeling about yourself or something you did.
  • gathered /ˈɡæðɚd/ — Came together in one place.
  • blocked /blɑkt/ — Stopped people or cars from moving past.
