The Great Gatsby — Chapter I, Section 2: Dinner Across the Bay (Abridged Reader)

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

My summer really began when I drove across the bay to have dinner with Tom and Daisy Buchanan. Daisy was my cousin, and I had known Tom at Yale. He had been a famous football player, the kind of man who reached the height of his success at twenty-one. His family was so rich that when he moved east from near Chicago, he brought his own polo ponies. Daisy said the move was permanent. I wondered whether Tom would ever be satisfied in any place for long.

Their red-and-white house stood above the water at the end of a huge lawn. Tom was waiting on the porch in riding clothes. He was about thirty, with a powerful body and a way of looking at you that made an ordinary conversation feel like a contest. He put a hand on my arm and turned me toward the garden and the motorboat floating offshore. He scarcely stood still long enough to enjoy it.

Inside, a breeze came through the open windows. It lifted the curtains and swept across the rug. Two women in white dresses seemed to float on a large couch. Then Tom shut the windows, and the room went still. Daisy reached for my hand and told me how happy she was to see me. Her low voice made you want to lean closer. Beside her was a young woman named Jordan Baker. She sat so still, with her chin lifted, that I almost felt rude for entering the room.

Daisy wanted to know if people in Chicago missed her. I told her, jokingly, that the entire city was in mourning. She laughed and said they should go back at once. Then, with no warning, she asked whether I had seen her two-year-old daughter. I said I had not. The child was asleep. Tom interrupted to ask about my work. He had never heard of my firm and made that sound like a judgment on the firm, rather than a limit to what he knew.

Jordan asked whether I lived in West Egg. She made it sound like an odd choice. Then she asked if I knew Gatsby. I told her he was my neighbor. Daisy turned toward us at the mention of his name, but a servant announced dinner. We went out to the porch, where the evening light still fell over the table.

At dinner Daisy held up a finger with a bruise on it and blamed Tom for hurting it, though she did not say he meant to. She teased him about his strength. Tom, in turn, began talking about a book that claimed white people were in danger of losing power. He insisted that its racist ideas were scientific. Daisy made fun of the importance he gave the subject, but Tom kept insisting that people like him had to protect their position. He sounded frightened underneath all his certainty.

Then a telephone rang inside. The servant whispered to Tom, who got up and left the table. Daisy followed him a moment later. I tried to continue the conversation with Jordan, but she wanted to hear what the others were saying. When I asked what was going on, she sounded surprised that I did not already know. Tom had a woman in New York, she told me. The caller should have had the good manners, Jordan added, not to call at dinner.

Tom and Daisy came back and tried to take their places as if nothing had happened. Then the telephone rang again. We all heard it. For a moment, the beautifully set table was the most uncomfortable place I could imagine being.

Vocabulary List

  • permanent /ˈpɝmənənt/ — Lasting for a long time and not changing.
  • satisfied /ˈsætɪsfaɪd/ — Happy because you have what you wanted.
  • contest /ˈkɑntɛst/ — A fight or competition to see who wins.
  • scarcely /ˈskɛrsli/ — Almost not at all.
  • mourning /ˈmɔrnɪŋ/ — Feeling very sad because someone has died.
  • interrupted /ˌɪntəˈrʌptɪd/ — Stopped someone while they were speaking.
  • judgment /ˈdʒʌdʒmənt/ — An opinion about whether something is good or bad.
  • bruise /bruz/ — A dark mark on the skin after you get hurt.
  • insisted /ɪnˈsɪstɪd/ — Said something strongly and did not change his mind.
  • certainty /ˈsɝtənti/ — Being completely sure about something.
  • uncomfortable /ʌnˈkʌmfərtəbəl/ — Feeling worried or not relaxed.
  • announced /əˈnaʊnst/ — Told people something in a clear, public way.
