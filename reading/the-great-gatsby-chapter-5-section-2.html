The Great Gatsby — Chapter V, Section 2: The Clock and the Rain (Abridged Reader)

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

Gatsby waited in my cottage, turning the pages of a book without really reading it. He looked at the wet windows and jumped at the sound of footsteps from the kitchen. At last he stood up and said he was going home. Nobody was coming, he insisted. I pointed out that it was still two minutes before four. Just then we heard a car turn into the lane.

I went outside. Daisy smiled at me from the car under a lavender hat, with a wet strand of hair across her cheek. She leaned toward me and asked softly whether I was in love with her or why else she had to come alone. I avoided the question and asked her to send her driver away for an hour.

When we went in, the living room was empty. Before I could figure out what to say, someone knocked at the front door. I opened it and found Gatsby in the rain, standing in a puddle with both hands deep in his pockets. He had slipped out the back. Now he came in through the front, as if he, too, had just arrived.

There was a silence. Then Daisy said she was very glad to see him again. Her voice sounded clear, but it took an effort to keep it that way. When I went into the room, Gatsby was leaning against the shelf above the fireplace, trying to look relaxed. His head rested against an old clock that no longer worked. Daisy sat on the edge of a chair. Gatsby managed to say that they had met before. Then the clock tilted, and he turned and caught it with shaking fingers. He put it back in its place and apologized. None of us seemed to know what to say next, even though nothing was broken.

Daisy said they had not seen each other for many years. Gatsby immediately gave the exact time: it would be five years in November. Then the tea arrived, and for a little while there were cups and cakes to talk about. Daisy and I kept the conversation going while Gatsby watched us from a dark corner of the room. I thought they might do better alone, so I got up to make an excuse and leave.

He followed me into the kitchen in a panic. The whole thing was a terrible mistake, he whispered. I said he was embarrassed and so was Daisy. He was astonished at the idea that she might be as uncomfortable as he was. I lost patience and pointed out that he was being rude by leaving her alone. He went back to her, and I went out the back door.

The rain was pouring again. I stood under a large tree for half an hour and looked at Gatsby’s house across my freshly cut, muddy lawn. A rich brewer had built the house and had wanted the neighbors to make their cottages look more like part of his estate. None had agreed. At last the rain stopped and sunshine appeared. I went back to the cottage, making enough noise in the kitchen to announce my return.

Neither of them seemed to hear me. They were on the couch, looking at each other. Daisy had been crying and got up to wipe her face at the mirror, but I saw no trace of their earlier embarrassment. Gatsby looked so happy that the room itself seemed brighter. When I mentioned that the rain had stopped, he repeated the news to Daisy as if it were a wonderful discovery. Then he asked both of us to come next door. At last he could show Daisy his house.

Vocabulary List

  • footsteps /ˈfʊtsteps/ — The sounds made by a person's feet when walking.
  • insisted /ɪnˈsɪstɪd/ — Said firmly that something was true.
  • puddle /ˈpʌdəl/ — A small pool of water on the ground.
  • fireplace /ˈfaɪərpleɪs/ — A place in a room where you can make a fire.
  • tilted /ˈtɪltɪd/ — Leaned to one side and was no longer straight.
  • shaking /ˈʃeɪkɪŋ/ — Moving quickly from side to side because of fear or cold.
  • embarrassed /ɪmˈbærəst/ — Feeling shy or ashamed because of something you did.
  • astonished /əˈstɑnɪʃt/ — Very surprised by something.
  • panic /ˈpænɪk/ — A sudden strong feeling of fear.
  • brewer /ˈbruər/ — A person who makes beer.
  • estate /ɪˈsteɪt/ — A large area of land with a big house on it.
  • trace /treɪs/ — A very small sign that something was there.
