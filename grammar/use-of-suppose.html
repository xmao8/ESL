Use of Suppose

Suppose is a small word with four common uses. It can mean “I think,” it can start a “what if” idea, and with be it talks about what people expect or what people say. The meaning changes with the form, so watch the form first.

1. I suppose = I think, I guess

Form: subject + suppose + (that) + clause. Use it for a soft opinion — you think something is true, but you are not 100% sure. It sounds more careful than “I think.”

  • I suppose it will rain tomorrow.
  • She supposes the bus is late again.
  • I suppose we should leave early to miss the traffic.
  • He supposes his boss will be happy with the work.

Short answers use so and not: “Will it rain?” — “I suppose so.” “Is the store open?” — “I suppose not.”

“What do you suppose …?” means “What do you think …?” — What do you suppose happened to him?

2. Suppose / Supposing = imagine, what if

Put Suppose or Supposing at the start of a sentence to imagine a situation that is not real: “what if …?” Then use the past simple for the imagined situation and would for the result — the same pattern as the second conditional.

  • Suppose you won a lot of money, what would you do?
  • Suppose it rained tomorrow, would you still go to the park?
  • Supposing you lost your job, where would you look for a new one?
  • Suppose I were you, I would take the morning class.
The past form talks about an imagined present or future, not the past: won, rained, and lost are about now, not yesterday.

3. Be supposed to = should, is expected to

Form: subject + be + supposed to + base verb. Use it for rules, plans, and what people expect — what you should do, or what is planned to happen.

  • You are supposed to wear a uniform at work.
  • The bus is supposed to arrive at 9:00.
  • We are supposed to finish this report today.
  • Students are supposed to speak English in class.

In the past, was / were supposed to often talks about something that did not happen — the plan and the real result are different.

  • I was supposed to call her, but I forgot.
  • He was supposed to be here at 10:00, but he came at 11:00.
  • They were supposed to bring lunch, but they bought fast food instead.

4. Be supposed to be = people say, people think

Form: subject + be + supposed to be + adjective / noun. Use it to report what people say or believe about someone or something — you are not sure yourself.

  • This restaurant is supposed to be very good.
  • He is supposed to be a great teacher.
  • That movie is supposed to be funny, but I did not laugh.
  • The new park is supposed to be the biggest one in town.

Common Mistakes

  • Forgetting “be” in “be supposed to”: supposed to needs am / is / are / was / were in front of it.
    Incorrect: I suppose to go now. Correct: I am supposed to go now.
  • Writing “I am suppose”: suppose is a verb (“I suppose …”), and supposed is the form used after be. Do not mix them.
    Incorrect: I am suppose it will rain. Correct: I suppose it will rain. / It is supposed to rain tomorrow.
  • Using “suppose” for “should”: When you mean a rule or an expectation, use be supposed to, not suppose alone.
    Incorrect: I suppose wear a uniform. Correct: I am supposed to wear a uniform.
  • Using the present form after “Suppose …”: For an imagined situation, use the past form, like the second conditional.
    Incorrect: Suppose you win a lot of money, what would you do? Correct: Suppose you won a lot of money, what would you do?

Summary

  • I suppose … is a soft “I think”: I suppose it will rain. Short answers: I suppose so / I suppose not.
  • Suppose / Supposing … starts a “what if” idea, with the past simple and would: Suppose you won a lot of money, what would you do?
  • Be supposed to + base verb means should or is expected to: You are supposed to wear a uniform. In the past it often means the plan did not happen: I was supposed to call her, but I forgot.
  • Be supposed to be + adjective / noun reports what people say: This restaurant is supposed to be very good.

Practice by making your own “suppose” sentences — one soft opinion, one “what if” question, one rule, and one thing people say is good.
