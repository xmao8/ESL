The Great Gatsby — Chapter V, Section 3: What the Green Light Meant (Abridged Reader)

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

While Daisy washed her face, Gatsby and I waited on my lawn. Sunlight lay across the front of his house. He asked how I liked it and said it had taken him only three years to earn enough to buy it. I reminded him that he had told me he inherited his money. He said he had lost most of it during the war. When I asked about his work, he answered sharply, then said he had been in the drug business and the oil business but was no longer in either. Daisy came out before we got any further. She loved the look of the house, though she could not imagine living there alone.

We walked around to the main entrance. The flowers smelled fresh after the rain, and all I heard on the marble steps was birdsong. I had never seen those steps so empty. Gatsby showed us music rooms, bedrooms, and a library. We surprised Klipspringer doing exercises in his pajamas. Gatsby watched Daisy instead of his possessions. The value of each room seemed to depend on the look it brought to her face.

Gatsby’s own bedroom was simpler than the other rooms, except for a set of gold things on the dresser. Daisy picked up a golden brush and smoothed her hair. Gatsby laughed and covered his eyes. First he had been afraid to meet her, then almost too happy to speak. Now he looked amazed that she could actually stand there and touch his things.

He opened two cabinets and showed us his clothes. A man in England sent him a new selection each spring and fall. Gatsby threw shirts onto the table, one after another. There were soft linen shirts and heavy silk ones, stripes and checks in green, orange, and lavender. The pile grew high. Daisy suddenly bent her face into it and began to cry. She said she had never seen such beautiful shirts. Why that should make her cry, she did not say.

We were going to see the grounds and the swimming pool, but the rain had returned. At a window, Gatsby told Daisy that without the mist they could see her house. He mentioned the green light that burned at the end of her dock. She took his arm. I wondered if he understood then that the light had changed. While Daisy was far away, it had been the closest thing to her that he could see. Now she was beside him, and the great green sign of all his hopes was only a light again.

I noticed a photograph of a man in sailing clothes. Gatsby said his name was Dan Cody and that he had once been his best friend. He also showed Daisy a collection of newspaper pieces he had saved about her. Then a telephone call interrupted him. He put down the receiver soon after Daisy called us to see a break in the clouds over the water.

Downstairs, Gatsby asked Klipspringer to play the piano even though he insisted he was out of practice. Gatsby and Daisy sat together across the dim room. As I came to say goodbye, I saw a puzzled look on Gatsby’s face. He had spent nearly five years building up the idea of Daisy. The woman beside him could not always be as wonderful as that idea, through no fault of her own. He took her hand, and she said something softly. He turned toward her again with all his attention. Her voice, with its own warmth and movement, was something he could never quite imagine in advance. They had almost forgotten I was there, so I went down the steps into the rain and left them together.

Vocabulary List

  • sharply /ˈʃɑrpli/ — In a sudden and unfriendly way.
  • selection /səˈlekʃən/ — A group of things chosen from a larger group.
  • linen /ˈlɪnən/ — Cloth made from a plant, often used for shirts.
  • stripes /straɪps/ — Long lines of a different color on cloth.
  • amazed /əˈmeɪzd/ — Very surprised by something wonderful.
  • possessions /pəˈzeʃənz/ — The things that you own.
  • mist /mɪst/ — A thin cloud close to the ground that makes it hard to see.
  • interrupted /ˌɪntəˈrʌptɪd/ — Stopped someone from speaking or doing something.
  • puzzled /ˈpʌzəld/ — Confused and not able to understand something.
  • fault /fɔlt/ — Something you did wrong, or a mistake.
  • warmth /wɔrmθ/ — A feeling of being warm, or of kindness.
  • earn /ɜrn/ — To get money by working.
