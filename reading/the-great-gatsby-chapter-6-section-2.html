The Great Gatsby — Chapter VI, Section 2: Visitors on Horseback (Abridged Reader)

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

For several weeks after the tea, I heard nothing from Gatsby. I was often in New York, spending time with Jordan and trying to make a good impression on her aunt. Finally I called on Gatsby one Sunday afternoon. I had hardly arrived when Tom Buchanan came in with two other people, a man named Sloane and a woman in brown riding clothes. All three had come on horseback and stopped at the house for a drink.

Gatsby said he was delighted to see them. He offered cigarettes and cigars, rang for drinks, and walked quickly from one guest to another. I could tell how much it affected him to see Tom there. The visitors did not show the same interest in him. Sloane refused everything that Gatsby offered. Gatsby reminded Tom that they had met before, in the city, and Tom said he remembered, although he did not look as if he did.

Then Gatsby said, with more force than he needed, that he knew Tom’s wife. Tom barely answered him. He turned to ask me where I lived and seemed surprised that I was right next door. For a while there was little conversation. The woman became friendlier after a couple of drinks and said they would come to Gatsby’s next party. He welcomed the idea and asked the three of them to stay for supper. Instead, the woman invited Gatsby and me to have supper at her place.

Sloane stood up to leave. I knew from his manner that he did not want us to come, so I said I could not. Gatsby, however, wanted to accept. The woman urged him again, even after Sloane spoke quietly in her ear. Gatsby explained that he did not own a horse. He said he would follow them in his car and went inside for a moment. When he had gone, Tom asked how Gatsby could fail to see that the woman did not really want him to come. I pointed out that she had said she did. Tom said Gatsby would not know anyone at the dinner, and then he began to wonder where Gatsby had met Daisy.

Sloane and the woman mounted their horses and told Tom they had to go. They asked me to explain that they could not wait. They rode off down the drive just as Gatsby came out with his hat and coat. He had taken an invitation at its word. They had expected him to understand that a polite invitation could mean something else.

The next Saturday, Tom came with Daisy to one of Gatsby’s parties. I suppose he was uneasy about letting her go there alone. The lights, the champagne, and the crowd seemed much the same as before, yet the evening felt different to me. Perhaps it was Tom’s presence. Or perhaps I had grown so used to West Egg that I needed Daisy there to see it through the eyes of someone who did not belong to it.

They arrived as the light was fading. Gatsby hoped to impress them with the people in his garden. He pointed out a beautiful movie star sitting beneath a white plum tree and the director bending over her. Daisy admired the star, but Tom said he did not know a soul there. Gatsby took them around the garden and introduced Tom as a polo player, although Tom objected. Gatsby seemed to enjoy calling him that, and he kept doing it. It was a little thing, and Tom tried to sound amused. Still, each of them had found a way to remind the other that he was not quite at home.

Vocabulary List

  • impression /ɪmˈpreʃən/ — The feeling or idea you get about someone or something.
  • delighted /dɪˈlaɪtɪd/ — Very happy and pleased.
  • refused /rɪˈfjuzd/ — Said no to something you were offered.
  • force /fɔrs/ — Strong power or energy.
  • barely /ˈberli/ — Only just, or almost not.
  • mounted /ˈmaʊntɪd/ — Got up onto a horse.
  • uneasy /ʌnˈizi/ — Worried and not comfortable.
  • fading /ˈfeɪdɪŋ/ — Slowly becoming less bright or strong.
  • objected /əbˈdʒektɪd/ — Said that you did not agree with something.
  • director /dɪˈrektər/ — The person who tells actors what to do in a movie.
  • admired /ədˈmaɪərd/ — Thought someone or something was very good or beautiful.
  • polite /pəˈlaɪt/ — Having good manners and showing respect.
