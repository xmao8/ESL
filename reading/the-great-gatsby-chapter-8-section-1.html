The Great Gatsby — Chapter VIII, Section 1: The Story Gatsby Told at Dawn (Abridged Reader)

I hardly slept that night. A foghorn kept sounding across the water. Near dawn I heard Gatsby come home. I got dressed and hurried next door. The great rooms looked different in the gray light without guests or music. We searched for cigarettes, and Gatsby told me that he had waited outside Daisy’s house until almost four. She had stood at her window for a moment, then turned out the light. Nothing else had happened.

I told him he ought to go away. The police could trace the yellow car, and people would have questions. He could travel for a week and let things settle. But the idea of leaving Daisy was unbearable to him. He said he had to know what she was going to do. I could see that telling him to forget her would make no more difference than telling him to forget the last five years of his life. Instead, I sat with him while he told me how it had all begun.

He was still an army officer when he met Daisy in Louisville, Kentucky. Other officers liked her too. Her house was as fascinating to him as she was. It had beautiful rooms upstairs, and there were flowers and fine furniture everywhere. It gave him a view into a kind of life he had never been allowed to know from the inside. Men had loved Daisy before him, and to Gatsby this made her seem even more valuable. He seemed to feel that their very interest in her told him what a prize he had won.

Gatsby had no money or family position to put beside hers. The uniform he wore allowed him to visit Daisy’s house on the same terms as the other officers. He let her believe he was someone from the same sort of world, someone who could look after her when the war was over. He had no secure place in that world at all, and he knew it. At first he had thought he might enjoy a short romance and go away. Instead, he fell in love and found that being with Daisy changed what he expected from his life.

When Gatsby went overseas, they wrote to each other. He did well in the war, but when it ended, he did not go straight home. He was sent to Oxford. Daisy wanted him back. His letters, she told him, left her feeling that he was far away and that no one could say when he would return. In Louisville, people were going out again. She could go to dances, meet a great many people, and make a choice about her future without waiting for news from another country.

Then Tom Buchanan came into her life. He was rich and confident. He was there, ready to marry her, when Gatsby was still far away. Daisy decided in favor of Tom, and the letter telling Gatsby about that decision reached him at Oxford. This was how Gatsby explained it to me. I had not seen her make that choice, and I could not tell what she had thought, alone with her letters. But I could hear the shock in his voice even now, as if he still expected to open the letter and find another ending.

The morning became brighter around us. For months I had heard other people’s stories about Gatsby, most of them invented. His own story did not clear away all the questions about him. Yet it helped me understand why he had built a house across the bay, why the parties had mattered, and why he would sit beside me that morning and still believe that Daisy might call.

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

Vocabulary List

  • foghorn /ˈfɔɡˌhɔrn/ — A loud horn that warns ships in fog.
  • trace /treɪs/ — To find something by following signs or clues.
  • settle /ˈsɛtəl/ — To become calm after trouble or excitement.
  • fascinating /ˈfæsəˌneɪtɪŋ/ — Very interesting and hard to stop thinking about.
  • valuable /ˈvæljuəbəl/ — Worth a lot of money or very important.
  • prize /praɪz/ — Something very good that you win or want to have.
  • secure /səˈkjʊr/ — Safe and sure, with no worry about losing something.
  • romance /roʊˈmæns/ — A loving and exciting feeling between two people.
  • expected /ɪkˈspɛktəd/ — Thought that something would happen.
  • overseas /ˌoʊvərˈsiz/ — In or to a country across the sea.
  • invented /ɪnˈvɛntəd/ — Made up stories that were not true.
