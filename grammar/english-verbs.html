English Verbs

A verb is the most important word in a sentence. It tells what the subject does, or what the subject is like. Every sentence needs a verb — without one, the sentence is not complete. Verbs come in different kinds, and each kind works in its own way.

1. Action Verbs

An action verb shows what someone or something does. The action can be physical, like run and cook, or mental, like think and decide.

  • She runs every morning.
  • They cooked dinner for their friends.
  • He thinks about the problem every day.
  • We decided to take the bus.

Most action verbs can be used in the continuous tenses: She is running now. They were cooking when I called.

2. State Verbs (Stative Verbs)

A state verb does not show an action. It shows a state — a feeling, a thought, a sense, or something you own. Common state verbs are know, like, love, hate, want, need, believe, understand, remember, and own.

  • I know the answer.
  • She likes hot tea.
  • They believe the story.
  • He owns a small shop.
State verbs are usually NOT used in the continuous tenses. We say I know him, not I am knowing him. We say She wants a new phone, not She is wanting a new phone.

3. Linking Verbs

A linking verb does not show an action. It links the subject to a word that describes the subject. The most common linking verb is be (am, is, are, was, were). Others are seem, become, feel, look, sound, taste, and smell.

  • She is a teacher.
  • The soup tastes delicious.
  • He seems tired today.
  • The music sounds beautiful.

After a linking verb, use an adjective (not an adverb) to describe the subject: She looks happy, not She looks happily. The word after the linking verb describes she, not the action of looking.

Some verbs can be both action verbs and linking verbs. Compare: She looked at the picture (action — she used her eyes) and She looked happy (linking — she seemed happy).

4. Helping Verbs (Auxiliary Verbs)

A helping verb works with a main verb to make a tense, a question, a negative, or the passive voice. The helping verbs are be, have, and do.

  • She is studying English. (is + main verb = present continuous)
  • They have finished the work. (have + main verb = present perfect)
  • Do you like coffee? (do + main verb = question)
  • He does not drive to work. (does + not + main verb = negative)

Modal verbs are a special group of helping verbs: can, could, will, would, shall, should, may, might, and must. They add meaning like ability, permission, or advice, and they are always followed by the base form of the verb.

  • You can swim very well. (ability)
  • May I come in? (permission)
  • You should study every day. (advice)

See also: Modals for Advice and Obligation.

5. Transitive and Intransitive Verbs

A transitive verb needs an object — the thing or person that receives the action. An intransitive verb does not take an object.

  • She bought a book. (transitive — a book is the object)
  • He opened the door. (transitive — the door is the object)
  • They arrived late. (intransitive — no object)
  • The baby slept all night. (intransitive — no object)

Some verbs can be both. Compare: She reads books every night (transitive) and She reads every night (intransitive — the object is not needed).

Do not put an object after an intransitive verb: He arrived at the station, not He arrived the station.

6. Verb Forms at a Glance

Most English verbs have five forms. The form changes with the tense and the subject:

  • Base form: work — I work every day.
  • Third person singular: works — She works every day.
  • -ing form: working — They are working now.
  • Past simple: worked — He worked yesterday.
  • Past participle: worked — We have worked here for years.

Regular verbs add -ed for the past forms. Irregular verbs change in other ways, like go — went — gone and eat — ate — eaten. For the full picture, see English Verb Forms and Irregular Verbs. For the tenses themselves, see English Verb Tenses.

Common Mistakes

  • Using a state verb in the continuous: State verbs like know, want, and like are not usually used with -ing.
    Incorrect: I am knowing the answer. Correct: I know the answer.
  • Using an adverb after a linking verb: After a linking verb, the word describes the subject, so use an adjective.
    Incorrect: She looks happily. Correct: She looks happy.
  • Forgetting the helping verb in a question or negative: In the simple present and simple past, questions and negatives need do / does / did.
    Incorrect: You like coffee? Correct: Do you like coffee?
  • “I am agree”: Agree is a verb, not an adjective, so it does not go after be.
    Incorrect: I am agree with you. Correct: I agree with you.
  • Putting an object after an intransitive verb: Verbs like arrive, sleep, and go do not take a direct object.
    Incorrect: He arrived the station. Correct: He arrived at the station.

Summary

  • Action verbs show what someone does: She runs every morning.
  • State verbs show feelings, thoughts, and things you own — usually not in the continuous: I know the answer.
  • Linking verbs (be, seem, feel, look, taste …) connect the subject to a describing word, and that word is an adjective: The soup tastes delicious.
  • Helping verbs (be, have, do) work with the main verb to make tenses, questions, and negatives. Modal verbs (can, should, must …) add meaning like ability or advice.
  • Transitive verbs take an object (She bought a book); intransitive verbs do not (They arrived late).
  • Most verbs have five forms: base, -s, -ing, past simple, and past participle.

Practice by sorting the verbs in a short story: which ones are action verbs, which ones are state verbs, and which ones are linking verbs? Then try making one question and one negative with each helping verb.
