The Great Gatsby — Chapter IX, Section 3: The Light Across the Bay (Abridged Reader)

After Gatsby’s funeral, I began to think about where I belonged. I remembered travel at Christmas, when I was a student and went home by train. We would meet friends at the station in Chicago. Then the train would carry us farther west, out past the lights of the small towns. When I saw snow at the windows and felt the cold air between the cars, I knew I was in my part of the country. It was not the wide fields I missed so much as the feeling of coming home. Gatsby, Daisy, Tom, Jordan, and I had all come from places west of New York. Perhaps none of us had ever understood quite how to live in the East.

Before I left, I spoke to Jordan. She was dressed to play golf and listened without moving while I tried to say what had happened between us. She told me she was engaged to another man. I was not sure I believed her. Then she reminded me that I had ended things with her over the telephone. She remembered that we had once talked about careless driving. A careless driver was safe, we had said, until she met another careless driver. She thought she had met one in me. I had been proud of being honest. I could not expect her to agree with me about that now. I left feeling angry, sorry, and still half in love with her.

Late in October, I met Tom on Fifth Avenue. He offered his hand, and I refused it. I asked him what he had said to Wilson on the day Gatsby died. Tom told me that Wilson had come to his house and wanted to know who owned the yellow car. Wilson had a gun in his pocket. Tom said he had told him the truth about who owned the car, and he claimed it was the only way to save himself. What he did not seem to know was that Daisy had been driving. I did not tell him.

I saw no way to make Tom understand what I thought he and Daisy had done. They had the money to leave whenever things became difficult. Other people had to stay and deal with what they left behind. I shook his hand at last because I could think of nothing else to say to him. Then he went into a jewelry shop. I went on down the street.

On my last night in West Egg, I went to Gatsby’s empty house. Someone had written an ugly word on the steps, and I rubbed it off with my shoe. Then I went down to the shore and looked across the bay. I imagined what Dutch sailors had seen when they approached this island long ago. The trees would have seemed like a promise of something new and wonderful. Gatsby had looked across the same water and seen a little green light at the end of Daisy’s dock. In his mind it seemed to show how close he was to a future he had wanted for years.

He did not understand that what he wanted was already behind him. Yet I could not look at the darkness and dismiss his hope. He had reached out for something that always seemed to be just ahead. It seemed to me that other people did the same thing, even if the light they followed was not green and the place they longed for was not across a bay. We keep moving toward the future we imagine, but the past keeps pulling at us. I thought of small boats trying to go forward against a current. They press on even as the water carries them backward.

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

Vocabulary List

  • belonged /bɪˈlɔŋd/ — Felt that a place was the right place for you.
  • engaged /ɪnˈɡeɪdʒd/ — Having promised to marry someone.
  • jewelry /ˈdʒuəlri/ — Pretty things like rings and necklaces made of gold or jewels.
  • approached /əˈproʊtʃt/ — Came closer to a place or a person.
  • imagined /ɪˈmædʒənd/ — Made a picture of something in your mind.
  • sailors /ˈseɪlərz/ — People who work on ships at sea.
  • forward /ˈfɔrwərd/ — Toward the front or toward the future.
  • rubbed /rʌbd/ — Moved your hand or foot over something again and again.
  • honest /ˈɑnəst/ — Telling the truth and not cheating anyone.
  • promise /ˈprɑməs/ — Something good that you hope will happen.
  • longed /lɔŋd/ — Wanted something very much.
