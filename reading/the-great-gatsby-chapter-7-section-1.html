The Great Gatsby — Chapter VII, Section 1: The Hottest Day of the Summer (Abridged Reader)

At first I noticed only that the lights at Gatsby’s house were off on Saturday night. A car stopped in the driveway, paused, and drove away. Another car did the same thing. For months people had arrived without invitations, sure there would be music and food. Now no one answered the door. I went over to ask if Gatsby was sick. The man who answered spoke rudely and shut the door before I could ask another question. He was not one of the servants I knew.

Gatsby telephoned the next day. He told me he had dismissed all his old servants. Daisy had been visiting him in the afternoons, and he wanted people in the house who would not gossip about her. His new servants had come through Meyer Wolfshiem. They knew little about keeping a large house, but they could keep a secret. The parties had ended just as suddenly as they had begun. Now that Daisy was coming to his house, the people who had danced there were no longer necessary to him.

Daisy called later and invited Gatsby, Jordan, and me to lunch at her house. The day of the lunch was almost unbearably hot. On the train even the seats felt hot. At the Buchanans’ house I found Daisy and Jordan lying on a large couch, dressed in white. They hardly moved. Tom was on the telephone in another room. Jordan guessed that his girlfriend was calling. I could hear him talk about selling a car.

When Tom left the room again to get drinks, Daisy crossed to Gatsby and kissed him. She did not seem embarrassed that Jordan and I were there. Soon a nurse brought in Daisy’s little daughter, Pammy. Daisy held the child for a minute and asked whether she liked the guests. Gatsby stared at the little girl. I had the feeling that he had never quite believed this child existed. He seemed to know how to imagine Daisy without Tom, but a living child was harder to leave out of the picture. The nurse led her away, and Tom returned with the drinks.

At lunch we talked about the heat and drank cold beer. Daisy asked what we would do that afternoon, the next day, and for the rest of our lives. Jordan told her that life started all over again when the air got cooler in the fall. Daisy suggested we go to New York. Then she looked across the table at Gatsby and told him he always looked so cool. The words were ordinary enough, but her eyes told him something else. Tom looked from one to the other. For the first time, he appeared to understand what was happening in front of him.

Tom got up and insisted that we go to the city. Daisy had been the one who suggested it, but now she did not want to hurry. As the others prepared to leave, Gatsby and I had a moment to speak outside. I tried to say what was unusual about Daisy’s voice, but the words escaped me. Gatsby said her voice was full of money. Suddenly I heard what he meant: it carried the easy confidence of a girl who had always lived among beautiful, expensive things.

We did not ride in the cars we had arrived in. Tom took Gatsby’s big yellow car, with Jordan and me beside him. Daisy went with Gatsby in Tom’s blue car. As we drove away, we left the other car behind. A little earlier, he had thought of Gatsby as an irritating neighbor. Now he knew he had a rival, and he was not going to let the matter rest.

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

Vocabulary List

  • dismissed /dɪsˈmɪst/ — Sent away from a job or a place.
  • gossip /ˈɡɑsəp/ — Talk about other people and their private lives.
  • embarrassed /ɪmˈbærəst/ — Feeling shy or ashamed in front of other people.
  • unbearably /ʌnˈbɛrəbli/ — In a way that is too strong or too hard to accept.
  • necessary /ˈnɛsəˌsɛri/ — Needed for something to happen or be true.
  • insisted /ɪnˈsɪstɪd/ — Said very strongly that something must happen.
  • rival /ˈraɪvəl/ — A person who competes with another person for the same thing.
  • confidence /ˈkɑnfədəns/ — A strong feeling that you can do something well.
  • irritating /ˈɪrəˌteɪtɪŋ/ — Making someone feel annoyed or angry.
  • ordinary /ˈɔrdəˌnɛri/ — Normal and not special in any way.
  • suggested /səɡˈdʒɛstɪd/ — Gave an idea for someone to think about.
  • escaped /ɪˈskeɪpt/ — Got away from a place or a difficult feeling.
