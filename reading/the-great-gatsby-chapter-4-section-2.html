The Great Gatsby — Chapter IV, Section 2: Lunch in New York (Abridged Reader)

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

As we drove through Astoria, a policeman on a motorcycle came up beside us. Gatsby slowed the car and held up a white card from his wallet. The policeman tipped his cap, apologized, and let us go. I asked what he had shown him. Gatsby said he had once done the police commissioner a favor and now received a Christmas card from him every year. Whatever the card said, it worked better than an explanation.

Sunlight flashed between the beams of the Queensboro Bridge. Across the river, the white shapes of buildings rose above the water. A funeral passed us with flowers piled high, and then a large car carried its passengers past us. From that bridge the city always seemed new to me, even after many crossings. It promised room for things I could not yet imagine. In a place like that, even Gatsby did not seem impossible.

At noon we went to a restaurant on Forty-second Street. After the bright street, I could barely see the man standing with Gatsby in the entrance. Gatsby introduced him as Meyer Wolfshiem. Wolfshiem was already in the middle of a story about someone and some money. He shook my hand, but he went right on talking to Gatsby, as though I had walked into a conversation that had begun long before I arrived.

At the table Wolfshiem said he preferred the old Metropole across the street. He had been there on the night his friend Rosy Rosenthal was killed. A waiter had told Rosy someone wanted him outside. Wolfshiem had tried to keep him in his chair, but Rosy stood up and went out anyway. His last concern, according to Wolfshiem, was that the waiter might take away his coffee. Men shot him on the sidewalk and drove away. To Wolfshiem, the old restaurant was still full of the memory of his friends.

Without a pause, he asked whether I wanted a business connection. Gatsby quickly explained that I was just a friend and not the man Wolfshiem had expected. Wolfshiem apologized and returned to his food, watching the room as he ate. I told Gatsby I still did not understand why he had to ask his favor through Jordan. He assured me there was nothing dishonest about it and said that Jordan would not help him do anything wrong.

Then Gatsby got up to make a telephone call. Wolfshiem told me he had known him since after the war and considered him a perfect gentleman. He also invited me to examine the buttons at his wrists. They were made, he said proudly, of human teeth. I looked at them and tried to think of something polite to say.

Wolfshiem left soon after Gatsby returned. When I asked what kind of work he did, Gatsby said he was a gambler. According to Gatsby, he had fixed the World Series in 1919. I had heard about that baseball scandal, but I had never pictured one man deciding to interfere with the game that so many people trusted. I asked why Wolfshiem was not in jail. Gatsby said the authorities could not catch him because he was smart.

I paid the bill, and as the waiter brought my change, I saw Tom Buchanan in the crowd. I took Gatsby over to meet him. Tom asked where I had been and said Daisy was angry that I had not called. He and Gatsby shook hands, but Gatsby suddenly looked uncomfortable. I turned toward him to explain that we had come in for lunch together. He had already disappeared.

Vocabulary List

  • commissioner /kəˈmɪʃənər/ — The head of a police department.
  • funeral /ˈfjunərəl/ — A ceremony held when someone has died.
  • connection /kəˈnekʃən/ — A person who can help you in business or in life.
  • dishonest /dɪsˈɑnɪst/ — Not honest, or not telling the truth.
  • assured /əˈʃʊrd/ — Told someone firmly that something is true.
  • examine /ɪɡˈzæmɪn/ — To look at something closely and carefully.
  • gambler /ˈɡæmblər/ — A person who plays games for money and risks losing it.
  • scandal /ˈskændəl/ — A shocking event that people talk about with disapproval.
  • interfere /ˌɪntərˈfɪr/ — To get involved in something and change it in a bad way.
  • authorities /əˈθɔrətiz/ — The people in charge, such as the police.
  • disappeared /ˌdɪsəˈpɪrd/ — Went away suddenly so nobody could see you.
  • introduced /ˌɪntrəˈdust/ — Made people meet each other for the first time.
