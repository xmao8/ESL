The Great Gatsby — Chapter VI, Section 3: The Past Gatsby Wanted (Abridged Reader)

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

Daisy and Gatsby danced together. I had never seen him dance before, and I was surprised by how graceful he was. Then they went over to my cottage and sat on the steps for half an hour. Daisy asked me to stay in the garden and keep watch, as if I were there in case of fire or flood. I think those thirty minutes were the best part of the evening for her.

At supper Tom asked permission to sit with some people at another table. Daisy offered him her little gold pencil in case he wanted to write down any addresses. She told me a moment later that the girl he was going to sit with was pretty, though not very refined. At our own table one woman had drunk so much that people had put her head in the pool. Her friends and a doctor began arguing. The same crowd might have amused me two weeks before. That night I saw what it looked like to Daisy, and it did not seem funny.

Near the end of the party we watched the movie director bend close and kiss the star’s cheek. Daisy still liked the actress, but almost everything else about the night had upset her. West Egg had grown from a fishing village into a place full of new money and people who displayed it. Daisy looked at it from the world she knew, and there was much about it she could not understand.

While we waited for their car, Tom asked whether Gatsby sold alcohol illegally. He had not heard anything definite, he admitted, but he intended to find out what Gatsby did. I denied the suggestion. Daisy said Gatsby had owned several drugstores and built up the business himself. She also defended him against Tom’s comments about the guests. Many of them had never been invited, she said. Gatsby was just too polite to turn them away.

After they left, I waited in the garden until Gatsby was free. It grew quiet; guests came back from the beach, and lights went out upstairs. When Gatsby finally came down, he said at once that Daisy had not enjoyed herself. I tried to make light of it, but he was sure. He felt far away from her, he said. He could not make her understand.

It was not really about a dance. Gatsby wanted Daisy to tell Tom she had never loved him. Then, once she was free, he wanted them to go back to Louisville and be married there, as if the years in between had never happened. I said he should not ask too much of her. I told him the past could not be repeated. He was amazed that I thought so. Of course it could, he said. He would put everything back the way it was.

As he spoke, I began to think he wanted to recover something of himself as well as Daisy. He told me about an autumn night five years earlier when they had stopped on a moonlit sidewalk. The blocks of stone had seemed to him like the steps of a ladder leading up into the stars. He might have climbed it alone, into a future he could still imagine any way he liked. Instead he waited as Daisy turned her face up to him. He kissed her, and from then on his dreams had her face. As he told me, I almost remembered a few words of something I had once heard. I opened my mouth to say them, but the words never came.

Vocabulary List

  • graceful /ˈɡreɪsfəl/ — Moving in a smooth and beautiful way.
  • refined /rɪˈfaɪnd/ — Polite and elegant, with good manners.
  • upset /ʌpˈset/ — Sad or worried because something bad happened.
  • displayed /dɪˈspleɪd/ — Showed something for people to see.
  • definite /ˈdefənət/ — Clear and certain.
  • intended /ɪnˈtendɪd/ — Planned or wanted to do something.
  • denied /dɪˈnaɪd/ — Said that something was not true.
  • defended /dɪˈfendɪd/ — Spoke in support of someone or something.
  • recover /rɪˈkʌvər/ — To get back something that was lost.
  • repeated /rɪˈpitɪd/ — Happened or was done again.
  • moonlit /ˈmunlɪt/ — Lit by the light of the moon.
  • sidewalk /ˈsaɪdwɔk/ — The path at the side of a road for people to walk on.
