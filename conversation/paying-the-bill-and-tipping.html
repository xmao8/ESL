Paying the Bill and Tipping

Characters:

  • MIKE: A friendly server at a local diner.
  • ELENA: A recent immigrant having her first restaurant meal in the US.

Scene: Elena just moved to the US and is having lunch at a diner. She’s confused about tipping. Mike, the server, brings the check.

Conversation

MIKE: Here’s your check, Elena. No rush — take your time.

ELENA: Thank you, Mike. Um… can I ask you a question? This is my first month in the US, and I’m confused about tipping.

MIKE: Of course! It confuses a lot of visitors. In the US, servers earn most of their income from tips.

ELENA: So the tip isn’t included in the bill?

MIKE: Usually not. For a table of one or two people, you add the tip yourself. The standard is 15 to 20 percent of the total.

ELENA: Let me see… my total is $24.50. So 20 percent would be about $5?

MIKE: Exactly! You’re a fast learner. Most people just double the tax as a shortcut, but your math is perfect.

ELENA: What if the service is bad? Do I still tip?

MIKE: Honestly, most people still leave at least 10 percent, unless something was really wrong. If there’s a problem, it’s better to talk to the manager.

ELENA: Good to know. Your service was wonderful, so you’ll definitely get 20 percent.

MIKE: Thank you! That really means a lot.

ELENA: Now, how do I pay? Can I add the tip to my credit card?

MIKE: Yes. I’ll run your card for the $24.50, then you write the tip amount on the receipt and sign it.

ELENA: What if I want to leave cash instead?

MIKE: That works too. Some people pay the bill by card and leave the tip in cash on the table.

ELENA: I think I’ll do that — it feels more personal. Here’s $30. Keep the change.

MIKE: Thank you so much, Elena! That’s very generous.

ELENA: You deserve it. One more question — do you tip at fast-food places too?

MIKE: Not usually. Tipping is mainly for sit-down restaurants, food delivery, taxis, and places like hair salons.

ELENA: This has been such a helpful lunch — great food and a free culture lesson!

MIKE: Haha! Come back anytime. Next time, I’ll teach you about splitting the check with friends.

ELENA: It’s a deal. See you soon, Mike!

— THE END —

Vocabulary List

  • Check /tʃek/ (n.) the bill in a restaurant. Example: Here’s your check.
  • Tip /tɪp/ (n.) extra money given for good service. Example: The standard tip is 15 to 20 percent.
  • Income /ˈɪnkʌm/ (n.) money earned from work. Example: Servers earn most of their income from tips.
  • Standard /ˈstændərd/ (adj.) normal or expected. Example: The standard is 15 to 20 percent.
  • Shortcut /ˈʃɔːrtkʌt/ (n.) a quicker way to do something. Example: Most people double the tax as a shortcut.
  • Run your card /rʌn jɔːr kɑːrd/ (phr.) to charge a credit card. Example: I’ll run your card for $24.50.
  • Receipt /rɪˈsiːt/ (n.) a paper showing what you paid. Example: Write the tip on the receipt and sign it.
  • Keep the change /kiːp ðə tʃeɪndʒ/ (phr.) let the worker keep the extra money. Example: Here’s $30 — keep the change.
  • Generous /ˈdʒenərəs/ (adj.) giving more than expected. Example: That’s very generous.
  • Split the check /splɪt ðə tʃek/ (phr.) to divide a restaurant bill among people. Example: Next time I’ll teach you about splitting the check.
