The Great Gatsby — Chapter VII, Section 2: A Room at the Plaza (Abridged Reader)

Tom had to stop for gasoline at George Wilson’s garage. Even the dust seemed tired in the heat. Wilson looked weak. He told Tom he had discovered something disturbing and wanted to take his wife away from the East. He needed money to move and still hoped to buy a car Tom had promised to sell him. Wilson did not know that Tom was the man Myrtle had been seeing. Looking at the two men, I realized that both had just learned something about their wives, and both were afraid of losing them.

Above the garage, I noticed Myrtle at a window. She was watching our yellow car. Her eyes settled on Jordan beside Tom, and her face was full of jealousy. I understood that Myrtle thought Jordan was Tom’s wife. Daisy and Gatsby, riding in Tom’s blue car, passed us on the road. Tom drove fast after that. Neither Daisy nor Myrtle seemed to be within his control now.

In New York we took a room at the Plaza Hotel. We thought we might escape the heat there, but the room became another place to sit and wait for the air to move. The sounds of a wedding rose from below. Tom asked Gatsby what he meant by coming to his house and using his familiar manners. Then he questioned Gatsby about Oxford. Gatsby explained that officers had been offered a chance to study there after the war, and that he had stayed for only five months. For a moment I felt glad he had answered with something plain and believable.

But Tom turned to the matter that had brought us all there. He wanted to know what Gatsby was trying to do to his family. Gatsby said that Daisy did not love Tom and never had. She had married Tom, he said, only because Gatsby had been poor and she had grown tired of waiting. Daisy tried to quiet both men. Gatsby asked her to tell Tom that she had never loved him. She repeated the words at first, with difficulty. Then Tom reminded her of moments they had shared, and she could no longer say what Gatsby wanted her to say.

“I did love him once,” she told Gatsby. “But I loved you too.” Gatsby seemed shocked by the idea that both things could be true. He wanted the five years they had spent apart to disappear. Daisy could not change what she had felt in the past. Tom heard the doubt in her voice and grew more certain of himself. He told her that she was not going to leave him.

Then Tom said he had learned about Gatsby’s business. He claimed that Gatsby and Wolfshiem had used drugstores to sell alcohol illegally. Gatsby did not answer in a way that put Daisy at ease. She watched the two men, and she drew farther away from Gatsby with every new accusation. I thought he was still trying to defend himself when the hope in his face began to fade. Daisy wanted to go home. Tom told her to take Gatsby and return in Gatsby’s car. He sounded certain there was no danger in letting them go together now.

After they left, I suddenly remembered that the day was my thirtieth birthday. While everyone else had been fighting about old loves and future plans, I had forgotten what day it was. Thirty had once seemed a distant age. Now the two of them were already on the road home, and I was sitting in a hotel room, thinking about how little I knew of what lay ahead.

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

Vocabulary List

  • disturbing /dɪˈstɜrbɪŋ/ — Making someone feel worried or upset.
  • jealousy /ˈdʒɛləsi/ — An unhappy feeling when someone else has what you want.
  • control /kənˈtroʊl/ — The power to make someone or something do what you want.
  • familiar /fəˈmɪliər/ — Known to you because you have seen or heard it before.
  • believable /bɪˈlivəbəl/ — Easy to believe because it seems true.
  • accusation /ˌækjuˈzeɪʃən/ — A statement that someone has done something wrong.
  • defend /dɪˈfɛnd/ — To protect someone or to say they did not do wrong.
  • doubt /daʊt/ — A feeling that you are not sure about something.
  • shocked /ʃɑkt/ — Very surprised by something sudden and bad.
  • illegally /ɪˈliɡəli/ — In a way that is against the law.
  • distant /ˈdɪstənt/ — Far away in time or in place.
