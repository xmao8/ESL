The Great Gatsby — Chapter II, Section 1: Beyond the Valley of Ashes (Abridged Reader)

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

The road from West Egg to New York runs near the railroad for a short distance. Here, between the rich communities of Long Island and the busy city, lay a place I called the valley of ashes. The gray waste from the city piled up on the ground. In the distance, men moved through the dust with shovels. Even the men looked as if they were made of ash.

Above the valley stood an old advertisement for an eye doctor named T. J. Eckleburg. Two enormous blue eyes stared through a pair of yellow glasses. There was no face, only the eyes and the glasses, fading in the sun and rain. The doctor who put up the sign had long since stopped looking for customers here, but his painted eyes still looked down over the road.

One Sunday afternoon, Tom Buchanan and I were riding into New York when he suddenly grabbed my arm. He told me to get off with him. He wanted me to meet his girlfriend. He spoke as if it had never occurred to him that I might have somewhere else to go. I followed him along the road to a small, yellow brick building. Most of the neighborhood seemed empty, but there was an all-night restaurant and, at the far end, a garage.

The garage belonged to George Wilson, a pale, fair-haired man. I could see no customers and only one car: a dusty old Ford in a corner. George brightened when Tom walked in. He wanted to know when Tom would sell him a car they had discussed. Tom said his man was still working on it. When George suggested the work was taking a long time, Tom threatened to sell it to somebody else. George quickly took back his complaint.

Just then a woman came down the stairs. Her name was Myrtle Wilson. She was George's wife, but for a moment she seemed not to see him. She looked straight into Tom's eyes as she shook his hand. Myrtle did not have the kind of beauty people turn to admire in a photograph. What I noticed was the life in her face and voice, as though energy were stored just below her skin.

Myrtle sent George to get chairs. As soon as he stepped away, Tom told her to take the next train and meet him by the newsstand in New York. She agreed quietly. By the time George came back, two chairs in his hands, Myrtle had already moved away from Tom. I asked Tom later what George thought of her visits to the city. Tom said George believed she was going to see her sister. Tom seemed amused.

Myrtle sat in another railroad car so that anyone from East Egg would not see her with Tom. In New York, she bought magazines and perfume before choosing a taxi with lavender paint. We had only just started to drive when she saw a man selling puppies from a basket. She told the driver to stop. She wanted a dog for the apartment. The seller said the puppy he held up was a kind of terrier and asked ten dollars. Tom paid him and told him to go away. The puppy settled in Myrtle's lap.

I tried to leave the two of them on Fifth Avenue, but Tom refused to hear of it. Myrtle asked me to come along and meet her sister Catherine. The next thing I knew, our taxi had stopped at an apartment building on West 158th Street. Myrtle stepped out with her magazines, her perfume, and her new dog.

Vocabulary List

  • advertisement /ˌædvɚˈtaɪzmənt/ — A sign or message that tries to sell something.
  • fading /ˈfeɪdɪŋ/ — Slowly becoming lighter or weaker.
  • grabbed /ɡræbd/ — Took hold of something quickly and firmly.
  • occurred /əˈkɝd/ — Came into his mind as an idea.
  • brightened /ˈbraɪtənd/ — Became happy and full of light.
  • threatened /ˈθrɛtənd/ — Said he would do something bad if he did not get his way.
  • complaint /kəmˈpleɪnt/ — Words that say you are unhappy about something.
  • admire /ədˈmaɪɚ/ — To look at someone with respect and pleasure.
  • energy /ˈɛnɚdʒi/ — The power to be active and lively.
  • lavender /ˈlævəndɚ/ — A light purple color.
  • terrier /ˈtɛriɚ/ — A small kind of dog.
  • refused /rɪˈfjuzd/ — Said no and would not do it.
