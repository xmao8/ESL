The American Yawp — Chapter One: American Origins
Abridged for Intermediate-High Learners

Long before Europeans crossed the Atlantic, the Americas were already home to millions of Indigenous people. They spoke hundreds of languages, built different kinds of political systems, and created cultures suited to hundreds of different environments. Some lived in small family groups, while others built great empires. Long-distance trade networks carried salt, pottery, shell jewelry, and food across the continents.

When the Spanish arrived in 1519, they could hardly believe what they found. Tenochtitlan, the capital of the Aztec Empire, held perhaps 250,000 people and 70,000 buildings. The city was built on a lake and connected by canals and causeways. One Spanish soldier said it looked like an enchantment. Meanwhile, in the Mississippi Valley, the city of Cahokia had more people than London in the 1100s. Because the Bible never mentioned these lands or their peoples, many Europeans were puzzled by their existence.

Indigenous peoples tell their own origin stories through dances, songs, paintings, and weavings. These stories pass sacred knowledge from one generation to the next, and they teach the deep connection between all living beings.

Scientists who study the past tell a different part of the story. Using evidence from archaeology, languages, and genetics, they believe people from Siberia in Asia arrived along the Pacific coast between 24,000 and 15,000 years ago. Lower sea levels exposed a wide stretch of open land called Beringia, where giant animals grazed on grass. Human footprints at White Sands in present-day New Mexico show that people walked there 23,000 years ago.

As the climate warmed about 19,000 years ago, glaciers melted and sea levels rose. People followed fish, animals, and new plant life across the continent. On the Pacific coast, skilled travelers settled where salmon and shellfish were plentiful. On the Great Plains, hunting communities followed bison and caribou herds, moving with the seasons. Everywhere, people developed their own ways of life in mountains, deserts, prairies, and forests.

Around 950 CE, during a warmer period, many communities turned more fully to farming. Indigenous women especially learned to domesticate wild plants and grow new crops. In the Southwest, farmers grew the Three Sisters: corn, beans, and squash. The Maya in Mexico were the first to develop corn from wild grasses, about 8,700 years ago. Corn was high in calories and easy to dry and store, so it spread across the continent and made larger settlements possible.

Farming supported impressive cities and monuments. At Poverty Point in Louisiana, people built giant earthen structures aligned with the stars and traded goods brought from as far as 800 miles away. The Olmec, about 3,200 years ago, created the Long Count calendar, developed an early form of writing, and learned to carve huge stone heads of their rulers. Teotihuacan in central Mexico grew to at least 25,000 people, with great pyramids dedicated to the sun and moon. In the desert near present-day Phoenix, Arizona, the ancestors of the Pueblo people built more than 800 miles of canals to water their fields. At Chaco Canyon in New Mexico, the stone rooms of Pueblo Bonito housed hundreds of people, until a severe drought forced the community to scatter.

Cahokia became the greatest city north of Mexico. Around 1050 CE, as many as 30,000 people lived there, near the meeting of the Mississippi, Illinois, and Missouri Rivers. At its center stood Monks Mound, an earthen hill whose base was larger than the pyramids of Egypt. But by 1300 CE, Cahokia began to collapse, likely because of warfare and political tensions made worse by a changing climate.

By the end of the 1400s, about 100 million people lived in the Americas, twice the population of Europe. When Europeans arrived, they found not an empty land but a world already full of peoples, cities, trade, and history.

Vocabulary List

  • indigenous — native people who lived in a place first, before others arrived.
  • empire — a large area ruled by one powerful leader or government.
  • enchantment — a magical and wonderful sight or experience.
  • archaeology — the scientific study of past human life through objects left behind.
  • genetics — the study of how parents pass traits to their children.
  • domesticate — to tame wild plants or animals so people can use them.
  • sacred — holy; connected to religion or spiritual belief.
  • drought — a long period of time with little or no rain.
  • scatter — to move or spread quickly in different directions.
  • mound — a raised pile of earth or stones.
  • pottery — objects such as pots and dishes made from baked clay.
  • jewelry — small decorations worn on the body, like rings and necklaces.
  • plentiful — existing in large amounts; easy to find.
  • carve — to cut a shape or design out of stone or wood.
  • collapse — to fall apart or fail suddenly.

True or False

  3. Tenochtitlan held about 250,000 people when the Spanish arrived.

Discussion Questions

  1. Indigenous peoples have their own origin stories, and scientists have evidence-based theories about how people arrived in the Americas. Why do you think it is valuable to learn both?
  2. Farming allowed cities like Cahokia and Teotihuacan to grow, but it also created new problems. What are some benefits and problems that farming brought? Do you think the change was mostly positive or negative? Why?
