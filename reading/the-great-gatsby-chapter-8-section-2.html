The Great Gatsby — Chapter VIII, Section 2: The Last Thing I Said to Him (Abridged Reader)

There was one more thing Gatsby told me about Louisville. After Daisy and Tom had married, he had gone back there for a short visit. He spent the last of his army pay to make the trip. He walked along the streets where he and Daisy had once gone together. He looked at the places that had seemed wonderful when she was beside him. The city was the same in many ways, but Daisy was gone. When his train pulled away, he watched as long as he could. It was hard for him to accept that a place could hold so much of his life and yet offer him nothing now.

We ate breakfast together. Gatsby returned to what Daisy had said at the Plaza. He still did not think she had ever really loved Tom. Then he admitted that she might have loved Tom briefly, just after their marriage. He seemed able at last to allow this possibility, but only if he could turn it into something that did not count. What mattered, he said, was that she loved him. I could see how badly he needed that to be true. I could not make the rest of her life disappear for him.

Before I left, the gardener came to say that he was going to empty the swimming pool. Leaves would soon be falling into it, he explained. Gatsby asked him to wait until the next day. He said he had not used the pool all summer. He had spent so long living as if something important were about to happen that he had hardly used the things he owned.

I knew I would be late for work, but I was reluctant to leave. Gatsby told me he supposed Daisy would call. As I walked toward the gate, I turned and called back to him. The people he had been trying to please, I told him, were a rotten crowd. He was worth more than all of them put together. He smiled. It was the only real compliment I ever gave him. I did not approve of everything he had done, and he knew it. But I was glad afterward that I had said it.

At the office, I found it hard to concentrate. Jordan telephoned. We spoke stiffly and quarreled about the night before. The call ended, and I could not be sure which of us had hung up first. Later I tried several times to reach Gatsby, but his line was busy. The operator told me it was being kept open for a call from Detroit. I decided to take a train back to West Egg that afternoon.

While I was in the city, I did not know how the night had passed at the garage. I learned afterward that Michaelis had stayed with Wilson for several hours. Wilson could hardly speak at first without crying out. Michaelis brought coffee and tried to quiet him. Wilson had found a little dog leash among Myrtle’s things. Michaelis did not see why the leash mattered. To Wilson, it meant his wife had been keeping a secret.

Wilson began putting that clue together with the yellow car. He said he had a way of finding out who had owned it. Michaelis tried to tell him it might simply have been an accident and that he should not guess. But Wilson was convinced that Myrtle had run out to speak to someone she knew. Wilson could think of nothing else until he found out who it was.

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

Vocabulary List

  • briefly /ˈbrifli/ — For a short time only.
  • possibility /ˌpɑsəˈbɪləti/ — Something that may happen or may be true.
  • reluctant /rɪˈlʌktənt/ — Not wanting to do something.
  • compliment /ˈkɑmpləmənt/ — Kind words that praise someone.
  • approve /əˈpruv/ — To think that something is good or right.
  • concentrate /ˈkɑnsənˌtreɪt/ — To give all your attention to one thing.
  • quarreled /ˈkwɔrəld/ — Had an angry argument with someone.
  • convinced /kənˈvɪnst/ — Sure that something is true.
  • leash /liʃ/ — A rope or strap used to hold and control a dog.
  • quiet /ˈkwaɪət/ — To make someone become calm and stop crying or talking.
  • stiffly /ˈstɪfli/ — In a cold and unfriendly way.
