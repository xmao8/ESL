Wish + Past Simple — Wishes About the Present

Wish + past simple talks about a wish for the present — something that is not true now, but you want to be true. Use the past simple after wish, even though you mean now, not the past. It is the perfect grammar for Thanksgiving: talking about the people, places, and things you are grateful for — and the things you wish were different.

1. Wish + past simple — wishes about the present

Form: subject + wish(es) + subject + past simple.

  • I wish I lived closer to my family.
  • She wishes she had more time to cook a big dinner.
  • They wish they spoke English better at the dinner table.
  • He wishes he were at home for Thanksgiving.

The past form talks about now: I wish I lived closer means I do not live close now, but I want to.

With be, use were for all subjects in formal English: I wish I were, she wishes she were. In everyday speaking, was is also common: I wish I was at home.

2. Wish + could — wishes about ability

Use could (the past form of can) to wish you were able to do something now.

  • I wish I could thank my teacher in person.
  • She wishes she could cook turkey like her mother.
  • We wish we could visit our parents every week.

Could here is not the past — it is a wish about now: I cannot thank my teacher in person today, but I want to.

3. Wishes about other people

Put a different subject after wish to talk about someone else: I wish + he/she/they + past simple.

  • I wish my son called more often.
  • She wishes her children visited every month.
  • We wish the dinner started earlier, so the children are not so hungry.

This is a soft way to say you want someone to do something — kinder than a direct order.

4. Wish + would — a short extension: requests and complaints

Use wish + subject + would + base verb when you want someone to change a habit, or when something annoys you. It is a polite request or a gentle complaint.

  • I wish you would call more often. (= Please call more often.)
  • She wishes he would help with the dishes after dinner.
  • I wish it would stop raining on Thanksgiving Day.
Use would for habits and actions that can change. Do not use it for wishes about yourself — say I wish I lived closer, not I wish I would live closer — and do not use it for things that cannot change.

Common Mistakes

  • Using the present form after wish: After wish, use the past form, not the present.
    Incorrect: I wish I live closer to my family. Correct: I wish I lived closer to my family.
  • Using “would” for a wish about yourself: Wish + would is for someone else’s habit, not for your own wish.
    Incorrect: I wish I would have more time. Correct: I wish I had more time.
  • Using “will” after wish: Never use will after wish for a present wish.
    Incorrect: I wish my son will call more often. Correct: I wish my son called more often.

Summary

  • Wish + past simple talks about a wish for now: I wish I lived closer to my family.
  • Wish + could talks about ability you want now: I wish I could thank my teacher.
  • Wish + someone else + past simple is a soft wish about another person: I wish my son called more often.
  • Wish + would is a polite request or complaint about a habit: I wish you would call more often.

Practice by making your own “I wish …” sentences about Thanksgiving — the people you love, the food you miss, and the things you are thankful for.
