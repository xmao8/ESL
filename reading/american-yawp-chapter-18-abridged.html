The American Yawp — Chapter Eighteen: The Progressive Era
Abridged for Intermediate-High Learners

By 1900, the wealth of the Gilded Age had brought terrible problems: corrupt politics, unsafe factories, dirty cities, and giant monopolies called trusts. A new generation of reformers and activists decided to fix them, giving the era its name — the Progressive Era. In 1911, a fire at the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory in New York killed 146 workers, most of them young women. The factory doors had been chained shut, and the owners were found not guilty. Across the country, workers went on strike for better pay and safer conditions.

A journalist called a muckraker exposed corruption and suffering. In 1906, Upton Sinclair's novel The Jungle described the horrors of Chicago's meatpacking plants. Shocked readers demanded action, and Congress passed the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act. Religious leaders joined the cause through the social gospel movement, which taught that Christians should fight poverty and injustice.

Women's clubs gave each activist new power in public life. The Woman's Christian Temperance Union fought against alcohol, which reformers blamed for poverty and family violence. Jane Addams opened Hull House in Chicago in 1889, a settlement house offering schools, clubs, and health care to poor immigrants. Addams also fought for an eight-hour workday and limits on child labor, and she later won the Nobel Peace Prize. The greatest goal was women's suffrage — the right to vote. Alice Paul led a militant wing of the movement whose followers marched, picketed the White House, and went to jail. In 1920, the Nineteenth Amendment finally gave American women the vote.

Progressive presidents also attacked the trusts. A trust was a monopoly — one company or group controlling a whole industry. John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil and Andrew Carnegie's steel companies crushed competitors and bribed politicians. President Theodore Roosevelt, who took office in 1901, won fame as a "trustbuster." His government broke up the Northern Securities railroad trust in 1904. Roosevelt believed some trusts were good and some were bad, so he preferred regulation to breaking companies apart. His successor, William Howard Taft, broke up even more trusts through the courts. In 1914, President Woodrow Wilson signed the stronger Clayton Anti-Trust Act.

Progressives also fought to protect nature, but they disagreed about how. John Muir believed in preservation: protecting beautiful wild places for their own sake. Conservationists like Gifford Pinchot wanted to use natural resources wisely for the greatest number of people. The two sides clashed over a dam in California's Hetch Hetchy Valley. Conservation won, and the valley was flooded to supply San Francisco with water. Meanwhile, hunters drove the passenger pigeon to extinction, and women in the Audubon Society helped win new laws protecting wildlife.

The Progressive Era brought little progress for Black Americans — in many ways, life grew worse. Southern states wrote new constitutions that used poll taxes, literacy tests, and grandfather clauses for the disenfranchisement of Black voters. In 1896, the Supreme Court ruled in Plessy v. Ferguson that "separate but equal" segregation was legal. Many middle-class Black Americans believed in racial uplift — improving their communities through education. But leaders disagreed about strategy. Booker T. Washington, founder of the Tuskegee Institute, urged Black Americans to build economic skills and accept segregation for now, a position his critics called accommodationist. W. E. B. Du Bois, the first Black American to earn a Ph.D. from Harvard, demanded full and immediate equality. Du Bois helped found the NAACP in 1909 and edited its magazine, The Crisis.

Progressive reform did not solve every problem, but it changed the relationship between Americans and their government. New laws protected workers, consumers, and nature, and women won the vote. When World War I pulled the nation's attention overseas, the Progressive Era came to an end.

Vocabulary List

  • reform — a change made to improve society.
  • muckraker — a journalist who exposes corruption and social problems.
  • trust — a group of companies that joins together to control an industry.
  • monopoly — complete control of a product or industry by one company.
  • strike — a refusal to work, organized to demand better conditions.
  • suffrage — the right to vote in elections.
  • amendment — a change or addition to a law or constitution.
  • preservation — protecting nature in its wild state for its beauty.
  • conservation — the careful and wise use of natural resources.
  • disenfranchisement — the act of taking away a person's right to vote.
  • segregation — the forced separation of people by race.
  • uplift — the idea of improving oneself and one's community.
  • accommodationist — a person who compromises with an unfair system instead of fighting it.
  • militant — using strong, forceful action to support a cause.
  • activist — a person who works hard to bring about social or political change.

True or False

  1. The 1911 Triangle Shirtwaist fire killed 146 workers after the factory doors had been chained shut.
  2. Upton Sinclair's novel The Jungle helped bring about new food safety laws in 1906.
  3. American women won the right to vote in 1869.
  4. Theodore Roosevelt believed that all trusts were harmful and should be destroyed.
  5. In Plessy v. Ferguson (1896), the Supreme Court declared racial segregation legal.

Fill in the Blanks

  1. Jane Addams founded House in Chicago in 1889.
  2. The Amendment, ratified in 1920, gave American women the right to vote.
  3. John wanted to protect wild places for their beauty alone.
  4. Booker T. Washington founded the Institute in Alabama.
  5. In 1904, Roosevelt's government broke up the Northern railroad trust.

Discussion Questions

  1. Some reformers wanted to break big companies apart, while others wanted the government to control them instead. Which approach do you think works better today? Why?
  2. Booker T. Washington wanted slow, practical progress, while W. E. B. Du Bois demanded full rights immediately. Which strategy do you agree with more? Explain your answer.
