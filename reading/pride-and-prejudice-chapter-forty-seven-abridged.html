Pride and Prejudice — Chapter Forty-Seven (Abridged)

As they drove home, Mr. Gardiner tried to comfort Elizabeth. "Thinking it over, I believe it's unlikely that Wickham would try something so dishonest against a girl staying with his own colonel's family. I want to hope for the best."

"Do you really think so?" Elizabeth asked, feeling a flicker of hope.

"I'm starting to agree," said Mrs. Gardiner. "Can you really believe Wickham capable of something so terrible?"

"I can believe he'd ignore almost anything — except his own interests," Elizabeth admitted. "But then why all this secrecy, if they truly meant to marry?"

Elizabeth then confessed she had known the truth about Wickham's dishonest character for months, ever since learning about his past from Mr. Darcy. She hadn't told anyone outside the family, believing it wouldn't matter once the soldiers left town. "If only we'd said something sooner," she said sadly, "none of this might have happened."

Worried and exhausted, they finally arrived home. Jane met Elizabeth with tears, admitting there was still no news of Lydia, though she hoped their uncle's help would fix everything. Mrs. Bennet, upset and dramatic as always, blamed everyone except herself, crying that the family would surely be ruined.

Mr. Gardiner calmly reassured her that he would go to London immediately to help find Lydia. Mrs. Bennet begged him to make sure Wickham married Lydia right away, worrying more about appearances than anything else.

At dinner, Mary — always serious — offered her own advice: "This is very unfortunate, but let it teach us a lesson. A woman's good reputation, once lost, can never be recovered." Elizabeth, too upset to respond, said nothing.

Later, alone with Jane, Elizabeth learned more details. Colonel Forster admitted he had noticed some attraction between Lydia and Wickham but hadn't been alarmed. Kitty confessed she'd actually known about their secret feelings for weeks but had kept quiet.

Elizabeth deeply regretted staying silent about Wickham's true character. "If we'd told everyone the truth, maybe this wouldn't have happened," she said sadly.

"Perhaps," Jane admitted. "But we didn't want to unfairly damage someone's reputation without being sure it was necessary."

Jane then showed Elizabeth the note Lydia had left behind for her friend, Harriet Forster.

Dear Harriet,

You'll laugh when you find out where I've gone! I'm off to marry the only man I could ever love — isn't it exciting? Don't tell anyone at Longbourn; I want it to be a big surprise when I write and sign my name "Lydia Wickham." Please apologize to Pratt for missing our dance tonight. Give my love to the Colonel, and wish us a happy journey!

Your loving friend, Lydia Bennet

"Oh, Lydia," Elizabeth sighed after reading it. "At least this proves she truly believed she was getting married. My poor father — how upset he must have been!"

Jane explained that their father had been completely silent with shock, while their mother immediately became ill from the stress. Their aunt and neighbors, including Lady Lucas, had come by to offer sympathy, though Elizabeth felt such visits were more painful than helpful.

Finally, Elizabeth asked what plans their father had made in London. Jane explained he intended to trace the couple's route carefully, starting from the last place they had switched carriages, hoping to discover where they had truly gone.

Vocabulary List

  • dishonest — not truthful or fair; deceptive.
  • flicker — a brief, small feeling or moment, such as hope.
  • secrecy — the act of keeping something hidden.
  • confess — to admit something, often something one feels guilty about.
  • dramatic — exaggerated or emotional in a noticeable way.
  • appearances — how something looks to other people, especially in terms of reputation.
  • reputation — the general opinion people have about someone's character.
  • alarmed — feeling sudden fear or worry.
  • regret — to feel sorry about something one has done or not done.
  • unfairly — in a way that is not just or reasonable.
  • sympathy — a feeling of care or sorrow for someone's trouble.
  • trace (verb) — to follow signs or evidence to find where someone went.
  • silent — not speaking; completely quiet.
  • route — the path or way taken to get somewhere.
  • painful — causing emotional or physical hurt.
