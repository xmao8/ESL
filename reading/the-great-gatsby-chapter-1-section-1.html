The Great Gatsby — Chapter I, Section 1: A New Start in West Egg (Abridged Reader)

When I was younger, my father gave me some advice. Before criticizing anyone, he said, remember that not everyone has had the advantages you have had. I took his advice seriously. I learned to listen before I decided what I thought of people. In fact, people sometimes told me more than I wanted to know. A person might begin a private story while I was hoping for nothing more than a quiet evening.

Still, there is a limit to how much a man can listen without making up his mind. When I returned from the East that autumn, I was tired of hearing other people's secrets. I wanted the world to behave better than it had that summer. Gatsby was the one exception. He stood for many things I disliked, yet he had an extraordinary ability to hope. Even now, I find it easier to explain what disappointed me about the people around him than to explain why he mattered to me.

My name is Nick Carraway. I grew up in the Midwest in a family that had been comfortable for several generations. We liked to tell stories about distant ancestors, but it was a relative who had started a hardware business who had really made the family prosperous. I went to Yale and served in the First World War. When I returned, the place I had always considered the center of my world suddenly seemed far away from everything. In the spring of 1922, I decided to move east and learn the bond business. Bonds were a kind of investment, and practically everyone I knew was going into finance. My father agreed to support me for a year while I got started.

I could have rented a room in New York City. But a man at the office suggested we share a small house on Long Island and travel into the city for work. He found us a weather-beaten house for eighty dollars a month. Then the company sent him to Washington, so I moved into the house alone. For a day or two, I felt like a stranger. One morning, a man on the road asked me how to get to West Egg village. I could tell him. It was a small thing, but afterward I walked on as if I belonged there.

I bought a stack of books about banking and investments. Their bright covers looked as promising as new money. I also planned to read for pleasure and to become the sort of person who was interested in everything. At that point, I had no idea how much of my attention would soon go to the people living on either side of a narrow bay.

From above, two pieces of land on Long Island look like giant eggs. They are about the same size and shape, but the people who live there consider them very different. East Egg is the more fashionable place. Its enormous white houses shine across the bay. West Egg has expensive homes too, but an address there does not impress people in quite the same way.

My little house stood near the tip of West Egg, between two enormous properties. The house on my right looked like a French mansion. It had a tower, walls partly covered in new ivy, a marble swimming pool, and more lawn than anyone could need. I did not know the owner yet, but I knew his name: Gatsby. My own house was ugly, but it was cheap and easy to overlook. And from there I could see the water and a little of my neighbor's lawn.

Adapted from F. Scott Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), in the U.S. public domain. Read the original at gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.

Vocabulary List

  • criticizing /ˈkrɪtɪsaɪzɪŋ/ — Saying that someone or something is bad.
  • advantages /ədˈvæntɪdʒɪz/ — Good things that help you do better than others.
  • exception /ɪkˈsɛpʃən/ — Someone or something that is different from the usual rule.
  • extraordinary /ɪkˈstrɔrdəˌnɛri/ — Very unusual and special.
  • prosperous /ˈprɑspərəs/ — Rich and successful.
  • fashionable /ˈfæʃənəbəl/ — Popular and in the newest style.
  • enormous /ɪˈnɔrməs/ — Very, very large.
  • promising /ˈprɑmɪsɪŋ/ — Likely to be good or successful in the future.
  • investment /ɪnˈvɛstmənt/ — Money you put into something to make more money later.
  • ancestors /ˈænsɛstɚz/ — The people in your family who lived long before you.
  • practically /ˈpræktɪkli/ — Almost completely.
  • overlook /ˌoʊvɚˈlʊk/ — To fail to notice something.
